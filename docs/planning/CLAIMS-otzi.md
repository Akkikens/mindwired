## D1 — The discovery + the man himself (Ötzi the Iceman)

- **[CONFIRMED]** On September 19, 1991, German holidaymakers Erika and Helmut Simon (from Nuremberg) discovered a human corpse at 3,210 m above sea level near the Tisenjoch/Giogo di Tisa in the Schnalstal/Val Senales valley, Ötztal Alps.
  — South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology, 'The discovery' — https://www.iceman.it/en/oetzi/the-discovery; Wikipedia 'Ötzi' — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96tzi
- **[CONFIRMED]** The Simons were descending from the Fineilspitze (Finail Peak) via the Niederjoch toward the Schnalstal and had left the marked path on a shortcut when they spotted the body, its head and shoulders protruding from ice and meltwater in a rocky gully.
  — German Wikipedia 'Helmut Simon (Bergsteiger)' — https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Simon_(Bergsteiger); Guidelines.org retelling — https://www.guidelines.org/sermons/the-strange-path-helmut-simon-took-to-god/
- **[CONFIRMED]** Helmut Simon had exactly one unexposed frame left on his roll of film and took a single photograph of the body lying face-down in the meltwater — the only image of Ötzi exactly as he was found by his discoverers.
  — James M. Deem, 'Information about Ötzi the Iceman' — https://www.jamesmdeem.com/books.ice.otzi.html; Guidelines.org — https://www.guidelines.org/sermons/the-strange-path-helmut-simon-took-to-god/
- **[CONFIRMED]** The Simons — and initially the authorities — assumed the body was the victim of a recent mountaineering accident (a modern climber), not a prehistoric man.
  — South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology — https://www.iceman.it/en/oetzi/the-discovery; Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96tzi

This first-impression detail appears in secondary accounts, not the museum's official narrative, and attributions vary.
  — Medium/SPQR 'Who Was Ötzi the Iceman' — https://medium.com/@ancient.rome/who-was-%C3%B6tzi-the-iceman-9cf14be1a6c1; Facts and Details — https://europe.factsanddetails.com/article/entry-109.html
- **[CONFIRMED]** On September 20, 1991, the first recovery attempt — by a mountain gendarme using a pneumatic chisel (jackhammer) and ice axes — damaged the body: it tore open the left hip/thigh region and shredded clothing before the tool ran out of compressed air; bad weather forced the attempt to be abandoned.   — Medical Daily Jun 2026 (https://www.medicaldaily.com/otzi-iceman-microbiome-living-yeast-study-477155); Scimex/RDWorld coverage of the Microbiome paper (https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/otzi-the-icemans-microbiome-still-showing-signs-of-activity-after-5300-years); genera independently confirmed by Gizmodo (https://gizmodo.com/otzi-the-icemans-microbes-still-show-signs-of-life-after-5300-years-2000766458) and Eurac Research (https://www.eurac.edu/en/magazine/otzi-and-his-microbiome-a-detailed-picture-of-the-microbial-community-associated-with-otzi)

- **[CONFIRMED 🧍]** In June 2001 — nearly ten years after the body was found — radiologist Dr. Paul Gostner of Bolzano General Hospital, working with pathologist Dr. Eduard Egarter Vigl, spotted a dense triangular mass in Ötzi's left shoulder on newly taken chest X-rays (taken to check a suspected rib fracture). Gostner estimated it was five times as dense as bone: stone. It was a flint arrowhead.
  — Smithsonian Magazine, "Testimony from the Iceman," February 2003 — https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/testimony-from-the-iceman-75198998/
- **[CONFIRMED]** The finding was announced on July 26, 2001: CT imaging revealed a ~2 cm flint arrowhead lodged under the left shoulder that had shattered the scapula. Notably, Gostner said at the time the Iceman "could have lived only a few hours after he was wounded" — the original 2001 read was HOURS, before the 2007 'minutes' narrative took over.
  — Deseret News / AP, "Ancient 'Iceman' killed by arrow, scientists say," July 26, 2001 — https://www.deseret.com/2001/7/26/19598160/ancient-iceman-killed-by-arrow-scientists-say/
- **[CONFIRMED]** The arrowhead had been missed for almost a decade because the wooden shaft was gone — earlier imaging (mid-1990s) showed something in the area that no one identified as an arrowhead.   — Smithsonian Magazine, Feb 2003 (URL above); Gostner P. & Egarter Vigl E., "INSIGHT: Report of Radiological-Forensic Findings on the Iceman," Journal of Archaeological Science 29 (2002); ACS ChemMatters — https://www.acs.org/education/chemmatters/articles/otzi-the-iceman-reveals-5300-year-old-secrets.html

  — CBS News, "Iceman Mystery Solved," Sept 17, 2002 — https://www.cbsnews.com/news/iceman-mystery-solved/ ; vs. Weber et al., Scientific Reports 15:42555 (Nov 27, 2025) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27969-9
- **[CONFIRMED 🧍]** In August 2005 the mummy underwent multidetector CT (16-row Siemens Sensation 16, 0.75 mm slices). Pernter, Gostner, Egarter Vigl and Rühli published the result in the Journal of Archaeological Science (vol. 34, 2007, pp. 1784-1786, "Radiologic proof for the Iceman's cause of death"): a 13 mm laceration of the dorsal wall of the left subclavian artery, a 3 mm pseudoaneurysm, and a large surrounding hematoma, with the arrowhead 6.5 mm from the vessel. Conclusion: death by hemorrhagic shock — "complete perimortem exsanguination."
  — AuntMinnie, "Cold case closed: CT solves iceman's cause of death" — https://www.auntminnie.com/clinical-news/ct/article/15582849/cold-case-closed-ct-solves-icemans-cause-of-death ; Pernter et al., J Archaeol Sci 34:1784-1786 (2007) — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229104583
- **[CONFIRMED 🧍]** The 'died in minutes' framing that dominated 2007-2024 traces to the 2007 team; paleopathologist Frank Rühli (University of Zurich) told press: "If the artery starts bleeding, it's a matter of minutes to die." This was the standard cause-of-death account for nearly two decades.
  — Live Science, "Cold Case Closed: 'Iceman' Mummy Bled to Death," June 2007 — https://www.livescience.com/1578-cold-case-closed-iceman-mummy-bled-death.html ; National Geographic, "Iceman Bled Out From Arrow Wound" — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/-iceman-bled-out-from-arrow-wound--x-ray-scan-reveals
- **[CONFIRMED 🧍]** THE HAND WOUND: Ötzi had a deep intravital cut on his right hand between thumb and index finger, reaching bone (it notched the second metacarpal). Wound-healing analysis (clotting/scab progression) dated it 3-8 days before death. Authors: Andreas Nerlich, Beatrice Bachmeier, Albert Zink, Stefan Thalhammer, Eduard Egarter Vigl. It is widely interpreted as an active-defense wound from close-quarters violence days before the arrow.
  — Nerlich et al., "Ötzi had a wound on his right hand," The Lancet 362, July 26, 2003 — https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(03)13992-X/fulltext
- **[CONFIRMED 🧍]** ATTACKED TWICE: a 2009 study (Andreas Nerlich with LMU Munich forensic scientist Oliver Peschel and Eduard Egarter Vigl) used immunohistochemical methods to show the hand wound was days older than the fatal trauma, while the arrow wound (and a blunt trauma to the back) was survived only briefly — evidence of at least two separate violent events, days apart. Nerlich: "Ötzi had only shortly survived the arrow wound and the blow on the back."
  — Nerlich, Peschel, Egarter-Vigl, "New evidence for Ötzi's final trauma," Intensive Care Medicine 35 (2009) — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00134-009-1409-4 ; ScienceDaily, Jan 28, 2009 — https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/01/090128074826.htm
- **[DISPUTED 🧍]** THE HEAD INJURY: a June 2013 proteomic study of brain tissue samples (Frank Maixner, Albert Zink et al., EURAC Bolzano with Saarland and Kiel universities) identified 502 proteins, 10 related to blood/coagulation, plus clotted blood cells — evidence the brain suffered bruising shortly before death, from either a blow to the head or a fall (CT had shown dark bruise-like spots). BUT the Nov 2025 clinical review counters: "Clear signs of an intravital TBI cannot be identified," no skull fractures — "the suspected cranial trauma cannot be confirmed." Treat perimortem head trauma as an open dispute.
  — ScienceDaily, June 10, 2013 — https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/06/130610084123.htm ; Live Science — https://www.livescience.com/37311-otzi-iceman-death-clues.html ; vs. Weber et al., Sci Rep 15:42555 (2025) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12663357/
- **[CONFIRMED 🧍]** MAY 2025 3D RECONSTRUCTION: Chiara Villa, Sara Larsen, Albert Zink and Niels Lynnerup (Int J Legal Med 139:2263-2271, online May 21, 2025; PubMed 40397133) re-segmented the 2013 CT scans in 3D (Mimics + 3ds Max forensic animation). Findings: arrowhead 2.6 × 1.6 × 0.6 cm lodged between the ribs and scapula; a rectangular 3.8 × 1.7 cm defect punched through the scapula; the left subclavian artery laceration confirmed in 3D; and — for the first time — the hematoma quantified at approximately 110 mL, a volume too small for immediate exsanguination ("death from blood loss alone may not have been immediate").
  — Villa et al., Int J Legal Med 139:2263-2271 (2025) — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00414-025-03510-5 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40397133/
- **[CONFIRMED 🧍]** MAY 2025 TRAJECTORY: the same paper reconstructs the shot as coming FROM BEHIND into the upper left back, the wound canal running forward, to the right and slightly DOWNWARD; the downward component implies the archer stood at higher elevation than Ötzi (alternatives: Ötzi kneeling, semi-upright, or bent forward).   — Weber et al., Scientific Reports 15:42555, Nov 27, 2025 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12663357/
- **[CONFIRMED 🧍]** NOVEMBER 2025 — WHAT ACTUALLY KILLED HIM (per the authors): the arrow was a contributing factor, but not instantly fatal.
