## DIMENSION 1 — The program and the machines (the failure ladder)

### Corrections (commonly-misreported facts)

- KOVTUNENKO FIRST NAME: The task brief says 'Vladimir Kovtunenko' — it is VYACHESLAV Mikhailovich Kovtunenko, chief designer of NPO Lavochkin 1977–1995 (Springer Solar System Research 2012, doi 10.1134/S0038094612070192). Script: 'Vyacheslav Kovtunenko' or just 'Lavochkin's new chief designer.'
- VENERA 13 TEMPERATURE: Widely misquoted as 465°C. Venera 13's site measured 457°C / 89 atm (Wikipedia infobox per NSSDCA); 465°C / 94 atm is VENERA 14's site. LePage quotes 465°C/89.5 atm for V13 — sources conflict, so soften to: 'around four hundred sixty degrees Celsius and roughly ninety atmospheres.'
- 'ONLY AUDIO EVER RECORDED ON ANOTHER PLANET' IS FALSE since Feb 2021 — NASA's Perseverance rover recorded audio on Mars (JPL release, Feb 22 2021, https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-perseverance-rover-provides-front-row-seat-to-landing-first-audio-recording-of-red-planet). Soften to: 'the FIRST sounds ever recorded on the surface of another planet — and for thirty-nine years, the only ones.'
- 'FIRST SOUND ON ANOTHER PLANET = VENERA 13' carries a live citation-needed flag on Wikipedia ('Venera 11 might have been the first' — its Groza acoustic experiment listened during the 1978 descent). Safe phrasing: 'the first sounds recorded ON THE SURFACE of another planet.'
- VENERA 7 'FOUND IN THE NOISE A WEEK LATER': the recovery is real but the timeline is split — LePage says the surface signal emerged 'over the course of the next couple of days' of processing; the Klaes 1993/Harvey 2007 account (via Wikipedia) says radio astronomer Oleg Rzhiga found the 23 minutes 'a few weeks later.' Never say 'exactly a week.' Soften to: 'only when engineers replayed the tapes — days, by some accounts weeks, later — did they find 23 more minutes of signal, at about one percent strength.'
- VENERA 7 SURFACE TEMPERATURE: use 475 ± 20°C (Wikipedia/Reeves; LePage says 474±20°C) — not the rounded 465°C some outlets use; pressure was not measured but CALCULATED at 90 ± 15 atm because a stuck telemetry commutator sent only temperature. Say 'about 475 degrees' and 'engineers had to calculate the pressure — the probe could only say how hot it was.'
- VENERA 7 IMPACT TIME: LePage says impact 05:34:10 GMT; Wikipedia (Reeves) says 05:37:10 UTC. Use 'just after 5:30 in the morning, Moscow receiving time Dec 15 1970' or omit the minute.
- VENERA 7 SURFACE TRANSMISSION LENGTH: 23 minutes is the standard figure (Wikipedia table, LePage); one Russian source (galspace) says ~20 minutes of the 53 total. '23 minutes' is defensible; 'about 23 minutes' is safest.
- VENERA 4 CRUSH ALTITUDE: LePage says transmissions ceased at ~26 km (06:13:17 GMT); NSSDCA-derived accounts say 24.96 km; Wikipedia program table says 'within 26 km.' Say 'roughly 25 kilometres above the surface — still higher than airliners fly.'
- VENERA 13 DESIGN LIFE vs SURVIVAL: 'designed for approximately 32 minutes, functioned at least 127 minutes' (Wikipedia V13/V14 both give 32-min planned life) — both numbers check out, but say 'at least 127 minutes' (contact ended when the relay orbiter set, not necessarily when the lander died).
- VENERA 15/16 ARE NOT '1984' MISSIONS: launched June 2 and 7, 1983; entered Venus orbit October 10 and 14, 1983; radar-mapped the northern hemisphere until July 10, 1984 (Wikipedia Venera 15 infobox per Siddiqi). Say 'launched in 1983, mapped into 1984.'
- DO NOT claim a Venera 13 lens-cap failure: the Wikipedia line 'only one of these worked for Venera 13' is contradicted in the same article (both ejected caps are visible on the ground — they ARE Ksanfomaliti's moving 'disk') and by the existence of both V13 color panoramas. The lens-cap curse belongs to Venera 9/10 (one of two failed on each) and Venera 11/12 (all failed). EXCLUDE.
- 'FIRST PHOTOS OF ANOTHER PLANET' (Venera 9): must be 'first photos FROM THE SURFACE of another planet' — Mariner 4 photographed Mars from flyby in 1965.
- HARD GUARDRAIL (restated): JWST cannot point at Venus — it is inside JWST's solar-exclusion field of regard. Never write 'JWST looked at Venus.' No source exists because it has never happened.
- 'CIRCULATING FANS' inside the landers: only the circulating-FLUID cooling system is well sourced (Venera 10, Wikipedia per Mitchell); Venera 1 'internal fans' carries a citation-needed tag. Say 'circulating coolant' unless mentallandscape.com (unreachable this session) is checked for the fan detail.
- 'SAPPHIRE CAMERA WINDOWS': unverified. Quartz windows are sourced for Venera 13/14 (Wikipedia: 'quartz camera windows...protected by lens covers that popped off after descent'). Drop 'sapphire' unless separately sourced.

- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 1 launched 12 February 1961 (00:34:36 UTC) — the first spacecraft to perform an interplanetary flight. Its last successful telemetry session was 19 February 1961 at 1.9 million km from Earth; the next scheduled session seven days later never happened. It flew silently within ~100,000 km of Venus on 19 May 1961. Suspected cause: an overheated solar-direction sensor. Jodrell Bank may have detected weak signals from it in June 1961.
  — Wikipedia 'Venera 1' (accessed 2026-08-16), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_1 (citing NSSDCA 1961-003A; EDN)
- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 3 launched 16 November 1965 (04:19 UTC); contact was lost 15 February 1966 (likely overheating — the same failure that killed Venera 2); the entry probe crashed into Venus on 1 March 1966, the first human-made object to impact another planet's surface. It carried medallions with the Soviet coat of arms and had been sterilized before launch over contamination concerns. Probable impact region 20°N–20°S, 60–80°E.
  — Wikipedia 'Venera 3', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_3 (citing Siddiqi, NASA SP-2018-4041 'Beyond Earth', p.1; Harvey)
- **[CONFIRMED]** In 1965 the Central Committee stripped the planetary-probe program from Sergei Korolev's OKB-1 and reassigned it to the Lavochkin bureau; Lavochkin's new ground-test regime found the Venera landers failed centrifuge tests at half the g-forces they were rated for.
  — Wikipedia 'Venera 3' History section, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_3
- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 4 launched 12 June 1967; its lander entered Venus's atmosphere 18 October 1967 (release at 04:34 GMT, entry at 10.7 km/s) and transmitted during a 93-minute descent — the first in-place analysis of another planet's atmosphere (it showed the atmosphere is dominated by CO2) — before transmissions ceased at 06:13:17 GMT at roughly 25–26 km altitude, the capsule crushed below its design depth.
  — Andrew LePage, 'Venera 4: Probing the Atmosphere of Venus', Drew Ex Machina, Oct 21 2017, https://www.drewexmachina.com/2017/10/21/venera-4-probing-the-atmosphere-of-venus/
- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 4's lander was engineered for 'pressures of at least 18 bars and temperatures up to 400°C' — because before the mission Soviet scientists believed the surface pressure was 'probably no more than about ten bars.' The real surface is ~92 bar and ~465°C.
  — LePage, Drew Ex Machina, Oct 21 2017, https://www.drewexmachina.com/2017/10/21/venera-4-probing-the-atmosphere-of-venus/ (modern surface values: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus)
- **[DISPUTED]** Soviet authorities initially claimed Venera 4 'stopped transmitting when it reached the surface' — a surface-arrival claim the Mariner 5 results (and later Soviet reassessment) contradicted; the probe died ~25 km up. (Dispute is the 1967 Soviet claim itself, not the modern consensus.)
  — LePage, Drew Ex Machina, Oct 21 2017, https://www.drewexmachina.com/2017/10/21/venera-4-probing-the-atmosphere-of-venus/; Inverse, 'Russia changed spaceflight forever — and lied about it a little', Oct 2022, https://www.inverse.com/science/venera-4-anniversary
- **[CONFIRMED]** NASA's Mariner 5 made its closest approach to Venus — 4,094 km above the surface at 17:34:56 GMT on 19 October 1967 — about 37 hours after Venera 4's entry; its remote sensing implied a surface pressure of 75–100 bars and temperature between 377°C and 527°C, confirming Venus's atmosphere was far denser than Venera 4 could survive.
  — Andrew LePage, 'The Return to Venus: The Mission of Mariner 5', Drew Ex Machina, Jun 15 2017, https://www.drewexmachina.com/2017/06/15/the-return-to-venus-the-mission-of-mariner-5/; NASA History, '55 Years Ago: Mariner 5 Explores Venus', 2022, https://www.nasa.gov/history/55-years-ago-mariner-5-explores-venus/
- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 5 (launched 5 January 1969) returned 53 minutes of atmospheric data on 16 May 1969 before being crushed at roughly 24–26 km altitude; Venera 6 (launched 10 January 1969) returned 51 minutes on 17 May 1969 and was crushed within ~11 km of the surface. Their hulls were strengthened to ~25 atm — still less than a third of the real surface pressure.
  — Wikipedia 'Venera 5' / 'Venera 6' / 'Venera program' (program table: 'crushed by pressure within 26 km / 11 km of the surface'), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_5, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_6, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_program

- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 7 launched 17 August 1970 and was 'massively overbuilt': a titanium pressure capsule designed for 180 bars and up to 540°C for 90 minutes, pre-chilled to −8°C by the interplanetary bus before entry.   — LePage, 'Venera 7: The First Landing on Another Planet', Drew Ex Machina, Dec 15 2020, https://www.drewexmachina.com/2020/12/15/venera-7-the-first-landing-on-another-planet/; Wikipedia 'Venera program' mission table (Kosmos 359), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_program

It was the first successful soft landing on another planet.
  — LePage, Drew Ex Machina, Dec 15 2020, https://www.drewexmachina.com/2020/12/15/venera-7-the-first-landing-on-another-planet/; Wikipedia 'Venera 7' (citing Reeves 1994, Huntress 2011), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_7

  — LePage, Drew Ex Machina, Dec 15 2020, https://www.drewexmachina.com/2020/12/15/venera-7-the-first-landing-on-another-planet/; Wikipedia 'Venera 7' (citing Klaes 1993; Harvey 2007), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_7

- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 8 launched 27 March 1972, landed 22 July 1972 at 09:32 UTC, and transmitted from the surface for 50 minutes 11 seconds.   — Wikipedia 'Venera 8', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_8
- **[CONFIRMED]** From Venera 9 (1975) onward the ~5-ton Proton-launched landers used a spherical pressure compartment to protect electronics, a shock-absorbing 'crush ring' landing cushion beneath, and a wide disk that looked like an antenna but was actually an aerobrake; they were designed to operate on the surface for a minimum of 30 minutes.
  — Wikipedia 'Venera program', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_program
- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 9 landed 22 October 1975 at 05:13 UTC near Beta Regio and returned the first images ever taken from the surface of another planet, operating 53 minutes until its relay orbiter set below the horizon. It measured ~90 atm, 485°C, and 14,000 lux of light. Its planned 360° panorama became a 180° panorama because one of its two camera lens covers failed to come off. Its orbiter had entered Venus orbit on 20 October 1975 — the first spacecraft to orbit Venus — acting as the lander's relay.
  — Wikipedia 'Venera 9', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_9; Wikipedia 'Venera program' ('first pictures ever taken on the surface of another planet')
- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 10 landed 25 October 1975 at 05:17 UT, ~2,200 km from Venera 9, and operated 65 minutes on the surface — enabled by a circulating-fluid heat-distribution system plus pre-cooling before entry. Like Venera 9, one of its two camera lens covers failed to come off, limiting it to a 180° panorama. It measured surface wind of 3.5 m/s.
  — Wikipedia 'Venera 10' (citing Mitchell; Siddiqi NASA SP-2018-4041 pp.128–129), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_10
- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 11 (launched 9 September 1978) landed 25 December 1978 and survived at least 95 minutes; Venera 12 (launched 14 September 1978) landed first, on 21 December 1978, and survived at least 110 minutes. On BOTH landers every camera protective cover failed to eject (cause never established) — zero images — and the soil-analysis experiment was lost to a leak that exposed its interior to full atmospheric pressure. They still returned data including evidence of lightning/electrical activity.
  — Wikipedia 'Venera 11' / 'Venera 12', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_11, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_12; Wikipedia 'Venera program' ('All four landers had problems with some or all of their camera lens caps not releasing')
- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 13 launched 30 October 1981 (06:04 UTC) and landed 1 March 1982 at 03:57:21 UT at 7.5°S, 303°E, just east of Phoebe Regio, hitting the ground at 7–8 m/s. Site conditions: 457°C and 89 atmospheres (per its Wikipedia/NSSDCA record; LePage gives 465°C/89.5). Designed for approximately 32 minutes, it functioned for at least 127 minutes — the longest any machine has ever survived on Venus.
  — Wikipedia 'Venera 13', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venera_13; LePage, 'First Pictures: Color Views of the Surface of Venus by Venera 13', Drew Ex Machina, Mar 1 2022, https://www.drewexmachina.com/2022/03/01/first-pictures-color-views-of-the-surface-of-venus-by-venera-13-march-1-1982/
- **[CONFIRMED]** Venera 13 returned the first color images from the surface of another planet — transmitting 11 full panoramas and 10 partial ones across its 127 minutes (1024×252-pixel scans digitized to 9 bits) — and recorded sound: Venusian wind, the lander hitting the ground, the pyrotechnic lens-cap ejection, and the drill at work.
