## ⚠ THE TITLE HAS TO CHANGE (correction #0)

The Icahn package locked **"4,000 Planes. Two Hours. One Order."** The research breaks two
thirds of it:

- **"Two hours" is false.** FAA/NARA put the sky clear at **12:16 p.m.** — **2 h 34 min**
  after the 9:42 order. The FAA's own bucket is "over 4,500 within the first three hours."
  **Say "under three hours," never two.**
- **"One order" is contested.** The Commission credits Sliney at 9:42. **The FAA's own
  institutional timeline says "At the direction of Transportation Secretary Norman
  Mineta"** at 9:45. Both are real (§4). The film should own that, not flatten it.
- "4,000" survives as a floor — but see correction #1.

**New primary title: "4,500 Planes Had to Land. There Was No Plan."**
Honest on all three axes, keeps the number, and the payoff — that no procedure existed —
is the actual thesis.

## ⚠ DATA CORRECTIONS (baked in — do NOT revert)

1. **"4,546" is UNVERIFIED and is not the FAA's number.** It comes from Zuckoff's *Fall and
   Rise* (2019) and Britannica, uncited; AOPA attributes it to "National Security Agency
   archives," which could not be confirmed. **The Commission says "about 4,500." The FAA
   says "more than 4,500."** Use those. If the exact-looking figure appears at all, it is
   "one widely repeated count puts it at 4,546," never as an agency figure. Competitors
   put 4,546 in their titles; we will not.
2. **Never say "4,545 of 4,546 complied" or "one plane refused."** Zuckoff's "all but one"
   could not be tied to any identified aircraft, and no primary source documents a single
   airborne non-complier. **Cut entirely.**
3. **No tape of the order's exact words has ever been published.** Every famous version —
   "Order everyone to land! Regardless of destination!" (USA Today 2002), "Clear the skies!"
   (Lynn Spencer, 2008), "That's it! I'm landing everyone!" (Zuckoff, 2019) — is a
   **journalistic or authorial reconstruction.** If voiced, label **RECREATION** and
   attribute on screen to its author. The narrator uses the Commission's wording: *land at
   the nearest airport*.
4. **Sliney did not "invoke SCATANA."** AOPA, Wikipedia and many videos say he did. The
   Commission never says SCATANA was invoked, and the CFR gives that declaration to the
   *military*, not to Herndon. Whether SCATANA/ESCAT was ever formally declared that day is
   **UNVERIFIED.** Say: "there was a Cold War plan, and it was not the plan they used."
5. **Gander did NOT take the most planes.** Halifax took the most aircraft (≈40-47);
   Vancouver took the most passengers (≈8,500). Gander is the story because of the *ratio*
   to town size — 38 aircraft and 6,656 people into a town of 9,651 — not because it was
   biggest.
6. **Operation Yellow Ribbon was not a standing contingency plan.** Transport Canada's own
   account: it was the code name given that day to the ad-hoc system for tracking the
   diverted aircraft. Do not present it as a plan that was activated.
7. **No Mexico figure exists.** Nothing beyond an uncited Wikipedia sentence. If mentioned
   at all: "a handful of South American flights continued to Mexico, whose airspace stayed
   open." **No number on screen.**
8. **Come From Away won ONE Tony — Best Direction of a Musical (Christopher Ashley). It did
   not win Best Musical.** And several of its characters are composites (§6).
9. **No Canadian clock time exists in the official record** for when Collenette's order was
   given. Transport Canada's booklet gives no times for the Canadian decisions. Do not
   invent one.
10. **Korean Air 85 complied — it did not refuse.** Its ACARS text carried "HJK"; when
    Anchorage asked it to squawk 7500 the crew **did**, which read as confirmation of a
    covert hijack. A compliance story that looked like defiance.

## ACT 1 — THE SYSTEM HAD NO PROCEDURE FOR THIS

- **[CONFIRMED — 32 CFR Part 245, 2001 edition (source 41 FR 9322, 4 March 1976)]** The plan
  in force that morning was **SCATANA — "Plan for the Security Control of Air Traffic and
  Air Navigation Aids."** What it actually contemplated: a Wartime Air Traffic Priority
  List, grounding VFR traffic, and **controlling or shutting down the navigation aids
  themselves** — VOR, VORTAC, TACAN, LORAN. Implementation was directed by *"appropriate
  military authorities"* — NORAD region commanders — upon an **Air Defense Emergency**.
- **[CONFIRMED — the thesis of Act 1]** It was built for Soviet bombers, and its defining
  act is **denying navigation aids to an enemy.** On 11 September that would have blinded
  4,500 friendly crews trying to find a runway. It contains **no civilian "land everything"
  instruction at all**, and its trigger belonged to the military, not to Herndon.
- **[CONFIRMED — Sliney 🧍, WBUR *On Point*, 11 Sept 2026, the single best line in the
  episode]** *"A ground stop technically is an order that prevents anyone from taking off.
  This was a tool that we did not have, but we adopted, which was to land everybody in the
  air, and whoever was left, we would deal with."*
- **[CONFIRMED-secondary — needs the Commission hearing transcript if quoted verbatim]**
  NORAD's **Gen. Ralph Eberhart declined to execute full SCATANA**, reportedly saying he
  would execute it *"once you have a modified SCATANA that… doesn't cause a bad situation."*
  DoD left every navigation aid running so the aircraft could land.
- **[CONFIRMED]** The only precedent for a planned national closure: **Operation Skyshield**,
  the early-1960s air-defence exercises. 9/11 was the first *unplanned* one.

## ACT 2 — THE LADDER OF STOPS (it was not one decision)

- **[CONFIRMED]** **~9:03/9:05** — New York Center declares **"ATC zero."** The Commission
  and SS17 say 9:05; Michael McCormick's own retelling says 9:03. **Use 9:05.**
- **[CONFIRMED — Rutgers full transcript]** **~9:04**, Boston Center on tape: *"We're gonna
  stop everybody, we're gonna shut Boston down, I'd suggest the same elsewhere."*
- **[CONFIRMED]** **9:06** — FAA ground stop on all traffic bound through New York airspace.
- **[CONFIRMED — Commission Ch.1 / SS17, and the sharper SS17 wording]** **9:25** — *"A
  manager at the Herndon Command Center asked FAA headquarters if they wanted to order a
  'nationwide ground stop.' While this was being discussed by executives at FAA
  headquarters, the Command Center **went ahead and ordered one anyway** at 9:25."* The
  Commission does not name the manager.
- **[CONFIRMED — Sliney, WBUR 2026]** *"When United 175 struck the tower that was when we
  ordered a national ground stop. At that point, no aircraft could take off."*
- **The distinction the whole episode turns on:** a **ground stop** keeps aircraft from
  taking off. It does nothing about the 4,500 already flying.

## ACT 3 — 9:42, AND THE MAN ON HIS FIRST DAY

- **[CONFIRMED — 9/11 Commission Report Ch.1, verbatim]** *"At 9:42, the Command Center
  learned from news reports that a plane had struck the Pentagon. The Command Center's
  national operations manager, Ben Sliney, ordered all FAA facilities to instruct all
  aircraft to land at the nearest airport. This was an unprecedented order. The air traffic
  control system handled it with great skill, as about 4,500 commercial and general aviation
  aircraft soon landed without incident."* (SS17 calls it *"a totally unprecedented order."*)
- **[CONFIRMED]** It was **his first day** as national operations manager — after ~27 years
  in air traffic control and FAA management. Say "first day in that job," never "rookie."
- **[CONFIRMED — Sliney, Cape News]** *"I was charged with managing that airspace; that was
  my charge. I have always felt very self-assured about what happened."* And: *"After the
  second aircraft hit the building, I didn't know how many more there could be. There could
  be 30 of them."*
- **[CONFIRMED — Sliney to AP, 24 April 2006]** *"When I issued that order to land
  regardless of destination, we were just trying to do something to try and resolve what was
  occurring and at least separate the good guys from the bad guys."*
- **[CONFIRMED]** No primary source says he sought or needed permission.
  **[ALLEGED — USA Today, 2002 reconstruction]** his supervisor **Linda Schuessler** 🧍
  *"simply nods"*, and headquarters — Garvey and Belger — *"with little discussion… approve"*
  **after** the fact. Label it as the newspaper's reconstruction.
- **[CONFIRMED — NARA, and a strong beat]** The **written** national advisory came later:
  **Advisory 036 at 11:06 a.m.** suspended operations in the National Airspace System. **The
  verbal order beat the paperwork by eighty-four minutes.**

## ACT 4 — ⭐ WHOSE ORDER WAS IT? (own this, don't flatten it)

- **[CONFIRMED — Norman Mineta, sworn testimony to the Commission, 23 May 2003]** *"at
  approximately 9:45 a.m.… I gave the FAA the final order for all civil aircraft to land at
  the nearest airport as soon as possible."* He was on open lines to Monte Belger and Jane
  Garvey.
- **[CONFIRMED — the FAA's own institutional timeline, 2026]** *"9:45 AM: At the direction of
  Transportation Secretary Norman Mineta, the FAA Command Center closed all U.S. airspace for
  the first time in history."*
- **[CONFIRMED]** The Commission's Chapter 1 credits the operational order to Sliney at 9:42
  and **does not mention Mineta in connection with it at all.**
- **[ALLEGED — USA Today 2002]** Mineta to Belger from the bunker: *"F— pilot discretion.
  Monte, bring down all the planes"* — minutes after Sliney's order.
- **[UNVERIFIED — Newsday's gloss, 2007]** that the Commission found Mineta gave the order
  *"about an hour after Sliney did."* **That sentence appears nowhere in the Commission's
  text. Do not repeat it.**
- **[UNVERIFIED — EXCLUDE]** The claim that Sliney was asked to back Mineta's account and
  refused. No primary source.
- **The honest framing:** two men gave a landing order within three minutes of each other.
  The Commission credits the operational one to the man on the floor; the Secretary's is the
  ratification from the bunker, which is why the FAA's own timeline reads *"at the direction
  of."* **Never say Mineta took credit falsely.** Mineta died in May 2022 — not a
  living-person risk, still a fairness one.

## ACT 5 — HOW 4,500 AIRCRAFT ACTUALLY CAME DOWN

- **[CONFIRMED — FAA's own account]** *"700 aircraft within four minutes of the order, 2,800
  in the first hour, and over 4,500 within the first three hours."*
  **[DISPUTED — the 700 window]** FAA says four minutes; **Sliney himself says ten**
  (History.com, 2 Sept 2021); Mineta said "within minutes." **Attribute, or say "within
  minutes."**
- **[CONFIRMED — FAA/NARA]** **Sky clear at 12:16 p.m.** — three and a half hours after the
  first impact, 2 h 34 after the order.
- **[CONFIRMED — Terry Biggio 🧍, Boston Center operations manager]** *"We told them, you're
  not leaving our airspace. Pick an airport."*
- **[CONFIRMED — Harvey Scolnick, Harrisburg Tower]** *"We had to make sure that every
  aircraft already in our airspace landed… within roughly half an hour, the scope went from
  being filled with traffic to being almost completely black."*
- **[CONFIRMED — Michael McCormick 🧍, New York Center]** *"I decided, we're under attack.
  They're using aircraft as weapons. The only way that I can work to stop it is to remove the
  weapons."* And, when later pressed to reopen: *"We are going to stay ATC zero, so you
  better find a way to deal with that."*
- **[CONFIRMED — John Carr, NATCA president]** *"The landing of those aircraft stands as the
  single greatest feat in all of ATC history."*
- **[CONFIRMED]** Exceptions: military, police, medical and lifeguard flights; from 12 Sept
  the FAA cleared organ-transplant charters.
- **[CONFIRMED]** **Korean Air 85** — see correction #10. F-15s shadowed it to **Whitehorse,
  Yukon**, on the ground about 2:54 p.m.; the town was partly evacuated. **[ALLEGED by
  Chrétien himself, to CBC a year later]** that he had authorised a shootdown in principle:
  *"I said yes, if you think they are terrorists… But be ready to shoot them down."*
  Attribute as "Chrétien told CBC a year later"; the 2002 interview was not viewed.
- **[CONFIRMED — no documented non-compliance]** Nothing anywhere records an airborne
  aircraft refusing. Winchester's *"none of significance appeared to balk"* is a historian's
  characterisation, not a record.

## ACT 6 — WHERE THE TRANSATLANTIC TRAFFIC WENT

**Primary exhibit:** Transport Canada, ***11-09-2001 Four Days in September*** (TP 13978,
2002) — the Canadian government's own after-action booklet. It says of itself: *"It is not
intended to provide a comprehensive account of the crisis… Nor is this booklet a
minute-by-minute account."* **Cite it as an official commemorative record, not an inquiry.**

- **[CONFIRMED — TC 2002]** **~500 transatlantic and 90 transpacific flights** were heading
  for North America; *"One to two planes entering Canadian airspace every minute."*
  **270 were ordered to turn back to Europe; 224 flights carrying 33,000+ passengers landed
  in Canada.** **[CONFIRMED — NAV CANADA's own count: 239 aircraft.]** Both are official and
  they differ. **Script says "some 225 to 240 aircraft" — never one bare number.**
- **[CONFIRMED — TC 2002]** The decision was executed by the Transport Canada Situation
  Centre under **Dr. John Read**, Acting ADM Safety & Security, *"with my approval"* —
  Collenette 🧍, who was driving back from Montreal with dead cell phones. Collenette:
  *"we did something no Canadian government had ever done — we essentially shut down our
  country's entire airspace."* And to Canadian Press in 2026: *"We had to make a decision on
  the spot. We had no hours. This was minutes."*
- **[CONFIRMED — TC 2002]** Read: *"This process took five minutes to complete."*
- **[CONFIRMED — TC 2002]** The code name: *"They code-named it Operation Yellow Ribbon. It
  was the system hastily set up to keep track of the 224 diverted planes."* (See correction
  #6.)
- **[CONFIRMED]** Distribution (NAV CANADA count): **Halifax 47 · Gander 38 · Vancouver 34 ·
  St. John's 21 · Winnipeg 15 · Toronto 14 · Calgary 13 · Stephenville 13 · Moncton 10** and
  more, across **17 airports**. ⚠ Halifax has four "official" figures (40, 44, 47) — say
  **"around forty"** and attribute any exact number.
- **[CONFIRMED]** **Gander: 38 aircraft, 6,656 people, into a town of 9,651** (2001 Census).
  A resident, quoted in TC's own booklet: *"On September 11th, we had 38 aircraft with a
  total of 6,656 people drop by for coffee, then stay for three or four days."*
  Mayor **Claude Elliott** 🧍: *"we tried to discourage people from taking them into their
  homes, but Newfoundlanders being Newfoundlanders, a lot of people didn't listen."*
- **[CONFIRMED]** Passengers were held aboard for hours — Halifax kept them on the aircraft
  *"until the evening of September 11th… cooped up for 16 hours or more."*
- **[CONFIRMED]** **Why Gander had a 10,200-foot runway:** built 1936-38 under a 1935
  agreement as the transatlantic refuelling stop — the last dry land before the ocean. RAF
  Ferry Command's main staging point in the war; "Crossroads of the World" in the 1950s; made
  redundant by 1960s long-range jets. **On 11 September 2001 the redundancy was the point.**
- **[CONFIRMED — the irony beat]** A national public-service strike was on that morning and
  the picket line encircled Transport Canada's own headquarters tower. When the union
  understood what was happening, *"they immediately stopped the picket lines and went back
  to work."* In Gander, striking school-bus drivers left the line to drive passengers
  (**attribute to DeFede/CBS**).

## ACT 7 — REOPENING, AND WHAT CHANGED

- **[CONFIRMED — DOT release, 12 Sept 2001]** A limited reopening, solely so **diverted
  flights could continue to their destinations**, *"Only passengers on the original flights"*
  aboard; curbside and off-airport check-in abolished.
- **[CONFIRMED — DOT statement, 13 Sept 2001]** Mineta *"has ordered the national airspace
  system re-opened to commercial aviation, effective at 11 a.m. Eastern time Thursday… only
  after they implement our more stringent levels of security."*
- **[CONFIRMED]** General aviation came back in phases over **months**: IFR 14 Sept with
  25-mile no-fly rings around Washington and New York; crop-dusting 18 Sept; most flight
  training 22 Sept; the enhanced Class B restrictions not lifted until **19 December**.
  **Reagan National did not reopen until 4 October.**
- **[UNVERIFIED — do not name a first flight.]** No source reached names a specific first
  departure on 13 September.
- **[CONFIRMED]** SCATANA was rewritten and renamed **ESCAT** — final rule 20 Oct 2006,
  effective **18 Jan 2007**, with the military retaining final authority once formally
  declared. ⚠ **ESCAT already existed in 2001** as a lesser phase inside SCATANA (a Canadian
  official heard an "ESCAT call" on the radio that day), so the wording is *"the Cold War
  plan was rewritten and renamed,"* **not** "a new plan called ESCAT was invented."
  ⚠ FAA Advisory Circular **99-1D has since been superseded by 99-1E** — say "since
  superseded" if it appears on screen.
- **[CONFIRMED]** CATSA — Canada's airport-security authority — **did not exist on 9/11**;
  it was created in response, in force 1 April 2002.
