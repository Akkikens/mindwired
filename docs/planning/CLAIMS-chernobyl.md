## ⚠ DATA CORRECTIONS (baked in — do NOT revert)

1. **The void coefficient — get the mechanism exactly right, not the pop-science
   shorthand.** As coolant water boils to steam ("voids"), the RBMK's specific
   graphite-moderator/water-coolant combination means losing water *increases*
   reactivity (opposite of Western reactors, where losing water reduces it).
   This is a stabilizing-vs-destabilizing feedback difference, not just "it got
   worse" — explain the actual physics once, precisely.
2. **The control-rod "tip effect"** — RBMK control rods had ~4.5m graphite tips
   below a boron-carbide absorber section, with a water gap below when fully
   withdrawn. Reinserting a fully-withdrawn rod first pushed the graphite tip in
   — displacing absorbing water and briefly *increasing* local reactivity — before
   the boron section could act. This is why pressing the AZ-5 emergency shutdown
   caused a power spike instead of a shutdown. Get this sequence right; it's the
   single most commonly garbled fact in pop coverage.
3. **INSAG-1 (1986) vs. INSAG-7 (1992) — the official story changed.** INSAG-1
   blamed the accident chiefly on operator violations. INSAG-7 revised this:
   "According to INSAG-1, the main cause of the accident was the operators'
   actions, but according to INSAG-7, the main cause was the reactor's design."
   Legasov's own 1986 Vienna presentation to the IAEA also leaned toward operator
   blame, matching INSAG-1 — not the later, corrected 1992 position. **This
   revision arc IS the episode's hook — never flatten it into "operator error"
   or "design flaw" alone.**
4. **Dyatlov's culpability is genuinely disputed — present both sides, assert
   neither.** He was convicted in 1987 and served ~3 years; he disputed personal
   culpability after release, arguing (consistent with what INSAG-7 later
   validated) that operators were never told about the void-coefficient/rod-tip
   flaws. Modern consensus treats it as shared causation: real design flaws
   operators didn't know about, compounded by real operator deviations from
   procedure. Never assert either side as sole cause.
5. **All named plant personnel are deceased** — Akimov (d. 10 May 1986, ARS),
   Toptunov (d. 14 May 1986, ARS), Dyatlov (d. 13 Dec 1995, radiation-linked

## ACT 1 — THE MACHINE AND THE FLAW (plant history, RBMK design, prior warnings)

- **[CONFIRMED]** Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant (V.I. Lenin NPP), Pripyat, Ukrainian
  SSR. Reactor 4 began commercial operation **20 December 1983** — barely over
  two years old at the time of the accident, the newest of the plant's four units.
- **[CONFIRMED]** RBMK-1000 design: graphite-moderated, water-cooled, no full
  Western-style containment building — only a partial "Accident Localization
  System." Built from mass-produced components (no need for a single forged
  pressure vessel) and refuelable online — also useful for producing
  weapons-grade plutonium, a real dual-use factor in why the USSR favored it.
  Designed principally at **NIKIET** under chief designer **Nikolay Dollezhal**
  (d. 2000), with physics/core input from the **Kurchatov Institute**.
- **[CONFIRMED — corrections #1/#2]** The positive void coefficient and the
  control-rod graphite-tip effect — the two design flaws at the technical heart
  of the disaster.
- **[CONFIRMED]** A real, documented **prior warning**: on 30 November 1975,
  Leningrad NPP Unit 1 (an earlier RBMK-1000) suffered a partial core-damage
  accident rooted in the identical void-coefficient physics — ~100 fuel channels
  damaged. Per an academic source (Malko, Kyoto University), this was never
  discussed in open Soviet forums, and the safety fix later recommended was
  only implemented **after** Chernobyl.

## ACT 2 — THE NIGHT (25-26 April 1986, per INSAG-7's own sequence)

- **[CONFIRMED]** The test: verifying whether a coasting-down turbine's residual
  momentum could power emergency cooling pumps during the ~40-50 second gap
  before diesel generators started — a real safety gap, tested unsuccessfully
  three times before (1982, 1984, 1985).
- **[CONFIRMED]** The test was delayed from daytime to a night shift because the
  Kyiv grid dispatcher needed the plant's power output — the reactor held at
  half power ~9 hours, pushing the test to a less test-experienced night crew.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Reactor power fell to a dangerously low, unstable level; to
  compensate, operators withdrew most control rods, disabling the plant's
  Operational Reactivity Margin safety procedure.
- **[CONFIRMED]** 01:23:04, 26 April 1986: the test began; automatic protections
  were bypassed to allow it to proceed. 01:23:40: shift supervisor **Alexander
  Akimov** ordered the AZ-5 emergency shutdown. Per the flawed rod design
  (correction #2), mass insertion caused a **localized power spike, not a
  shutdown** — power surged, rupturing fuel channels; two explosions followed
  (steam/fuel-fragmentation, then a hydrogen/steam or prompt-critical thermal
  explosion), blowing off the roof and igniting a graphite fire that burned
  ~10 days.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Named individuals, all deceased: **Akimov** (shift supervisor,
  d. 10 May 1986, ARS), **Leonid Toptunov** (senior reactor control engineer who
  executed the AZ-5 press, d. 14 May 1986, ARS, aged 25), **Anatoly Dyatlov**
  (deputy chief engineer overseeing the test, convicted 1987, d. 1995 —
  correction #4 on disputed culpability).

## ACT 3 — THE OFFICIAL STORY CHANGES (INSAG-1 → INSAG-7, the cover-up, Legasov)

- **[CONFIRMED — the core hook, correction #3]** INSAG-1 (1986, presented at the
  IAEA's Vienna Post-Accident Review Meeting) blamed operators. Legasov led the
  Soviet delegation and personally leaned this direction. **INSAG-7 (1992)**,
  after further investigation (Soviet/post-Soviet commissions chaired by N.A.
  Shteynberg and A.A. Abagyan surfaced design-flaw evidence Moscow had withheld
  in 1986), formally revised the primary cause to the reactor's design.
- **[CONFIRMED]** The cover-up sequence: Pripyat's ~49,000 residents were not
  evacuated for **>36 hours** (evacuation began 27 April, 2:00pm). Kyiv's May Day
  parade proceeded despite fallout (a real, if less-triple-sourced, detail that
  Ukrainian party chief Shcherbytsky reportedly wanted to cancel but was overruled
  from Moscow). International disclosure was forced by **Sweden's Forsmark plant
  detecting elevated radiation on 28 April 1986** — not voluntary Soviet
  disclosure.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Valery Legasov (chemist, Kurchatov Institute deputy director)
  led the technical investigation and the Vienna presentation. He secretly
  recorded five cassette tapes before his death — a real primary source, though
  self-recorded memoir rather than independently authenticated forensic evidence
  (translated transcripts are published; original audio is not freely available
  — see footage section). Found dead **27 April 1988** (correction #6), suicide,
  no note. Posthumously awarded **Hero of the Russian Federation, 20 September
  1996**.
