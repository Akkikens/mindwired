### A.2 The rudder system — the design decisions
- **[CONFIRMED]** Max deflection ±30°, rate 60°±5°/s, max pedal travel 4 in., **22-lb
  breakout force**; both seats' pedals rigidly linked. AAR p.18 + fn39.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Travel limiter (Table 2, p.21): 0–165 kt = 30°; 220 kt = 14.5°; **250 kt =
  9.3°**; 270 = 7°; 310 = 5°; 350 = 4°; ≥395 = 3.5°. Yaw-damper authority at 250 kt = 4.4°
  (10×(165/KIAS)²). AAR p.20.
- **[CONFIRMED]** **Table 3 (p.23):** 135 KCAS full rudder = 65 lb, 4.0 in., 30°. **250 KCAS
  full rudder = 32 lb, 1.2 in., 9.3°** — ~10 lb above breakout, about an inch of foot travel.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Airbus's two changes vs A300B2/B4 (§1.6.2.2, p.24): (1) pedal forces cut
  for "consistency" after control-wheel forces were reduced ~30% at pilots' request;
  (2) travel limiter switched from **variable ratio** to **variable stop** ("less complex
  and had less severe failure modes"). Result: "a substantial increase in the airplane's
  response to a given amount of force above the pedal breakout force at higher
  airspeeds"; sensitivity "increases with the square of the airspeed"; "twice as
  responsive to a pedal displacement at 250 knots KCAS than at 165 KCAS." AAR p.26.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Table 5 (p.27) at 250 kt — max/breakout force ratio · deg/lb · % pedal
  displacement to full rudder · work (lb-in): A300B2/B4 4.68 · 0.09 · 100% · 294;
  **A300-600 1.45 · 0.93 · 30% · 32**; B727 2.94 · 0.21 · 43% · 44; B747 4.21 · 0.20 ·
  100% · 198.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Finding 11: A300-600 "couples a rudder travel limiter system that
  increases in sensitivity with airspeed… with the lightest pedal forces of all the
  transport-category aircraft evaluated." Finding 14: "susceptible to potentially
  hazardous rudder pedal inputs at higher airspeeds." AAR p.160.
- **[DISPUTED]** "10× as sensitive as the B2/B4, 2× any other transport" — investigator
  Hess's analysis; Airbus and BEA disputed; not a Board conclusion (correction #6).
  https://flightglobal.com/no-winners/53231.article (403 on fetch; snippets only);
  https://avweb.com/leadnews/and-sensitive-points-about-sensitivity/
- **[CONFIRMED]** Airbus VP training at the public hearing: the rudder "is not a primary
  flight control to induce roll under any circumstances unless normal roll control is not
  functional." AAR p.23. Hearing: Oct 29–Nov 1, 2002, Washington DC, Acting Chairman Carol
  Carmody 🧍 presiding. AAR p.167 ("Acting Chairman Carol Carmody presided over" — [verified

### A.3 AAMP — American's Advanced Aircraft Maneuvering Program
- **[CONFIRMED]** Development began 1996 after an industry review of 1987–96 accidents
  found loss of control the leading cause; built as the upset module of AA's "selected
  event training" (FAA POI approved Aug 1, 1995). When written, >50% of AA's fleet were
  MD-80s/F.100s; AA "never thought" its widebodies "could end up in an upset from a
  'normal' encounter with wake turbulence." AAR pp.79–80, 84.
- **[CONFIRMED]** McDonnell Douglas chief test pilot "pretty much wrote the book" on the
  handling segment; "the rudder was the main area for discussion." AA presented AAMP at
  Boeing Seattle; Airbus was invited but "no test pilot or representative from Airbus
  attended." 2-day AAMP Industry Conference May 29–30, 1997, Dallas (FAA, NTSB, Boeing,
  MDC, Airbus, military). AAR p.81.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Ground school 6.5 h initial; recurrent = videotapes. **Captain attended
  AAMP ground school May 1997; F/O March 1997.** Simulator: captain Dec 1997 initial,
  recurrent Jul 1998/Jul 1999/Jul 2000/Jun 2001; **F/O Nov 1997 initial, recurrent Nov
  1998/Nov 1999/Dec 2000; next due end of Nov 2001** (~2 weeks after the crash). AAR pp.81–82.
- **[CONFIRMED]** The "excessive bank angle" simulator exercise: pilots told they were
  "following a heavy jet (some instructors specifically stated… a Boeing 747)"; sim rolled
  10° one way then past 90° the other, with ailerons/spoilers/rudder **inhibited until 50°
  bank or 10 s elapsed.** ASRS 1988–99: real wake upsets in large transports usually ≤30°,
  never >60°. AAR pp.82–83. A 🧍 (unnamed) instructor who gave the F/O his last sim
  training: aileron alone "would put themselves into a sideslip condition, so 'a little
  bit' of rudder was necessary." p.83.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Jan 1, 1997 booklet (the version the accident pilots received): "High AOA
  maneuvering = RUDDER"; "At the higher angles of attack, THE RUDDER becomes the most
  effective roll control." Sep 1, 1997 revision → "coordinated rudder." AAR pp.84–85.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Dec 19, 1997 AAMP video (course developer, taught to ~200 pilots Mar/Apr
  1997), verbatim: "if you jam full right rudder, that's the spin entry procedure, see?"
  The tape defined "coordinated rudder" as wheel and pedal the same direction (fn141).
  AAR p.86. Docket item #15 has 16 pp of booklet/tape excerpts (exhibit source).
- **[CONFIRMED]** Internal dissent: May 22, 1997 letter from AA's A300 technical pilot
  (named by AP/CBS as David Tribout) to Airbus's chief test pilot — AAMP "infers that
  aileron application in these situations is undesirable"; AP quotes "I am very concerned
  that one aspect of the course is inaccurate and potentially hazardous." Airbus chief
  test pilot replied May 23 he "shared" the concern. AAR p.87;
  https://cbsnews.com/news/doomed-flight-who-was-warned
- **[CONFIRMED]** May 27, 1997 memo, AA managing director of flight operations technical
  → chief pilot/VP flight: "grave concerns about some flawed aerodynamic theory and flying
  techniques that have been presented in the AAMP"; "exceptionally dangerous" to teach
  rudder as primary roll control; Boeing's chief test pilot "vehemently disagreed"; urged
  "immediate corrective action." **AA did not give this memo to the NTSB until Feb 6,
  2003.** AAR pp.88–89.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Aug 20, 1997 joint Boeing/Douglas/Airbus/FAA letter: AAMP "excellent"
  but "the excessive emphasis on the superior effectiveness of the rudder for roll
  control… is a concern." AA's chief pilot/VP flight (AP: Cecil Ewing) replied Oct 6,
  1997 that AA "does not advocate using 'rudder first' or 'rudder only.'" Airbus says it
  did not receive the reply until Jan 20, 1998. AAR pp.87–88 + fn143.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Simulator fidelity gap: Jan 31–Feb 2, 2002 tests on a real A300-600 at
  Tulsa vs Apr 23, 2003 in AA's sim — the sim's cable-stretch model was "less stiff"; in
  the Dec 4, 2002 study pilots making full-rudder inputs saw 10.3°–18.9° pedal travel vs
  the airplane's 7.9° limit at 240 kt and "did not have a sensation of going past a pedal
  stop." AAR pp.90–91.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Only in **early 2003** did AA train every A300 pilot that "certification
  standards do not account for a maneuver with alternating rudder inputs." AAR p.92.

### A.4 The precedent — Flight 903, May 12, 1997
- **[CONFIRMED]** AA903, A300-600 N90070, near West Palm Beach; 165 aboard, 1 serious + 1
  minor injury. Descending 24,000→16,000 ft at 230 kt; autothrottle levers slipped; speed
  decayed to 178 kt in a holding turn; bank past 50°, stickshaker; pilot "used full left
  rudder to arrest the roll"; **34 s of pitch/yaw/roll oscillations**; recovered ~13,000
  ft. NTSB ID DCA97MA049. AAR pp.106–107. Probable cause verbatim: "The flightcrew's
  failure to maintain adequate airspeed during leveloff which led to an inadvertent stall,
  and their subsequent failure to use proper stall recovery techniques. A factor
  contributing to the accident was the flightcrew's failure to properly use the
  autothrottle." (mirror: https://ntsb-reports.com/data/20001208X07893/1997-05-12-WEST-PALM-BEACH)
- **[CONFIRMED]** Airbus: 903's fin bending moment reached **1.53× the lateral-gust limit
  load** on the first alternating input; later inputs "estimated to have produced loads
  beyond ultimate load." Assessed by "engineering judgment"; no formal loads evaluation
  until early 2002. Jun 1997 paper trail: Daimler-Benz Aerospace "urgently recommended"
  fin inspection; Jun 19 Airbus memo — ultimate loads "could have been reached… including
  the vertical stabilizer"; Airbus e-mail to AA: airplane must be "deeply inspected"; AA
  reported the fin "OK." AAR pp.107–108.
- **[CONFIRMED]** **Mar 11, 2002:** NDI at Tulsa found delamination on N90070's right rear
  main attachment fitting — the same fitting that fractured on 587 — hidden under the lug
  pin; fin pulled, tested at Hamburg. AAR p.108.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Airbus service table (Table 8, p.106, as of Feb 2004): A300-600 — 4 fin
  events beyond limit load (3 with pedal input) in 6.69M fleet hours; A310 — 3 (3);
  A300B2/B4 — zero in 9.77M; Boeing reported none ever. Other precedents: Interflug A310,
  Feb 11, 1991, Moscow (alternating ~1/3 pedal → ultimate fin loads); **AA934, Oct 28,
  2002** (A300-600, FL310/290 kt — F/O "inadvertently kicked the left rudder pedal" →
  Dutch roll, "violent tail shift"). AAR pp.108–109.
- **[CONFIRMED]** The Board "declin[ed] to make a causal connection between the two events"
  (903 ↔ 587). AAR p.119 fn 167 (correction #19).

### B.5 Loads & structure
- **[CONFIRMED]** Design limit loads, A300-600 fin (Table 6, §1.6.4): shear −215,800 N;
  bending 861,650 Nm; torsion 152,680 Nm. 1986 A310 full-scale test fracture: shear
  −424,440 N, bending 1,677,700 Nm; lug fractured at ~905 kN (~2× limit lug force).
- **[CONFIRMED]** Estimated loads at separation (§1.16.2.3 p.59): shear 353,000–436,000 N;
  bending 1,580,000–1,840,000 Nm; torsion 18,600–48,100 Nm (±5%). **Finding 6 verbatim:**
  fin "fractured from the fuselage in overstress, starting with the right rear lug while
  the vertical stabilizer was exposed to aerodynamic loads that were about twice the
  certified limit load design envelope and were more than the certified ultimate load
  design envelope"; it "performed in a manner that was consistent with its design and
  certification." p.159. Post-accident lug tests at Hamburg 2003–04: 907 kN, 893 kN,
  >953 kN (§1.16.4.2 pp.67–68).
- **[CONFIRMED]** NASA-Langley wake study: JAL47 vortex ~100 s old, 63–80% initial
  circulation; author testified the wake was "nothing extraordinary" (p.56). Simulation:
  wake alone produced only ~10° bank deviation and <2.5° sideslip; the large oscillations
  "were the result of control wheel and rudder pedal inputs" (p.57).
- **[CONFIRMED]** Composite debate resolved: early investigation (Carmody, Nov 2001) openly
  examined composites (NYT Nov 19, 2001 "A Workhorse of the Skies, Perhaps With a Deadly
  Defect"); outcome — lugs failed in overstress at ~2× limit; Airbus composites specialist:
  design "met or exceeded all U.S. certification standards," validated by "more than 40
  million flight hours." NTSB press release Oct 26, 2004: "the composite material used in
  constructing the vertical stabilizer was not a factor in the accident because the tail
  failed well beyond its certificated and design limits"; "had the first officer stopped
  making inputs at any time before the vertical stabilizer failed… the accident would not
  have happened." https://web.archive.org/web/20131002135427/https://www.ntsb.gov/news/2004/041026.htm
