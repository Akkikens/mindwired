## 1. Origins & engineering (CONFIRMED unless noted)

- Program began as "Grand Tour"/TOPS, killed by Nixon's Jan 5 1972 budget cuts
  (traded against Space Shuttle funding); replaced by cheaper twin craft named
  **Mariner Jupiter-Saturn 1977 (MJS77)**, renamed **Voyager** by NASA
  Administrator James Fletcher on **March 7, 1977**.
- The ~175-year planetary alignment made 1977 the launch window.
- MJS77 development cost options were $210M/$260M/$320M (JPL, Jan 1972,
  CONFIRMED-SECONDARY); total mission cost through the interstellar mission =
  **$865 million**, +$30M for the post-Neptune extension (CONFIRMED, NASA
  fact sheet).
- **Ed Stone** was Voyager's sole project scientist **1972-2022 (50 years)**,
  also JPL director 1991-2001; died June 9, 2024, age 88. Strong figure to
  name in the origins chapter and again at the "still flying" close.
- **Voyager 2 launched first — Aug 20, 1977; Voyager 1 launched second — Sept
  5, 1977** on a faster, shorter trajectory (why the "1" craft launched
  second — a genuinely good "wait, what?" beat). Both flew Titan
  IIIE-Centaur rockets from Cape Canaveral.
- **Real near-failure at Voyager 1's own launch**: the Titan first stage
  burned an off-ratio fuel mix and shut down early; the Centaur upper stage
  came within **3.5 seconds of exhausting its fuel** compensating before
  reaching orbit (CONFIRMED-SECONDARY, space.com/AmericaSpace, corroborated
  by multiple outlets). Real, dramatic, almost nobody knows this — strong
  chapter-1 beat.
- **Built to last five years** (to reach Jupiter and Saturn) — NASA's own
  verbatim fact sheet language. The core contradiction for the whole episode.
- **Six onboard computers, three redundant pairs**: CCS (Computer Command
  System, 18-bit, 4,096 words), AACS (Attitude/Articulation Control, 18-bit,
  4,096 words), FDS (Flight Data System, 16-bit, ~8,198 words each) — combined
  ≈68 KB across all six. Clock 4 MHz, CPU throughput **~8,000
  instructions/second** (NASA's own comparison: a 2013 smartphone did 14+
  billion).
- **RTG power**: 3 MHW-RTGs per craft, ~158 W each = ~470 W total at launch.
  Fuel: Plutonium-238, half-life 87.7 years. Power now declines ~4 W/year;
  logged at **~225 W** as of Nov 2023 (V1)/April 2024 (V2) — down from 470 W
  at launch. Decay is **permanent and irreversible** — the reason instruments
  must be shut off one by one, forever, never restored (with the one narrow
  exception noted below re: the "Big Bang" fix).
- Nov 28, 2017: JPL fired Voyager 1's backup trajectory-correction thrusters
  for the first time **since Nov 1980 — 37 years dormant** — to fix
  degrading attitude control; worked exactly as commanded. Great "1970s
  engineering still works" beat.

## 2. The Grand Tour (CONFIRMED unless noted)

- **Jupiter (Voyager 1): closest approach March 5, 1979.** ~19,000 images.
  First-ever detection of Jupiter's ring system. Great Red Spot revealed as a
  dynamic, rotating storm system.
- **Io volcanism — the best human-discovery beat in the whole mission.** On
  March 9, 1979, JPL optical-navigation engineer **Linda Morabito** was
  processing a March 8 navigation image (stretching contrast to find faint
  background stars for spacecraft positioning) and spotted an umbrella-shaped
  plume rising ~270 km off Io's edge. Her team spent the day ruling out a
  camera artifact or unknown moon before confirming: an active volcano,
  later named **Pele** — the first active volcanism ever observed on a body
  other than Earth. Eight active plumes found in the full image set.
  (CONFIRMED — Morabito's own historical account, arXiv:1211.2554.) This is
  the strongest single "a real person discovered this, by accident, looking
  at something else" moment in the mission and deserves its own dedicated
  scene, named.
- **Saturn (Voyager 1): closest approach Nov 12, 1980.** Discovered ring
  "spokes," 5 new moons, revealed Titan's atmosphere as ~90% nitrogen, too
  thick to see the surface through.
- **The trajectory "sacrifice" — real, deliberate, not a myth.** Mission
  planners studied 10,000+ trajectories; a close Titan flyby (for the
  atmosphere science) was mutually exclusive with preserving a path onward to
  Uranus. NASA chose Titan. The flyby geometry bent Voyager 1 permanently out
  of the ecliptic plane — no further planetary encounters, ever. Voyager 2
  flew a deliberately different trajectory specifically to keep the
  Uranus/Neptune option open — which is why only Voyager 2 continued the
  Grand Tour. Precise, safe script line: "Voyager 1's path was traded for one
  close look at a single moon."
- **Voyager 2 continuing alone**: Saturn Aug 25, 1981 (real drama: ~100 min
  after closest approach, the camera scan platform's azimuth gearing SEIZED
  from a lubrication failure, knocking out imaging for days — engineers
  fixed it by Aug 28, 1981, CONFIRMED via NASA NTRS technical report
  19830062955 + contemporaneous Washington Post coverage). Uranus flyby **Jan
  24, 1986** — magnetic field tilted ~59° off the rotation axis, 11 new
  moons. Neptune flyby **Aug 25, 1989** — closest planetary approach of the
  whole mission (~4,800 km above the clouds); discovered the Great Dark Spot
  (winds ~2,100 km/h, fastest recorded in the solar system) and active
  **nitrogen geysers on Triton** (plumes climbing ~8 km on the coldest known
  surface in the solar system, ~38 K). JPL's own TV studio became "Neptune
  mission control" for the encounter; VP Dan Quayle visited the next morning.
- **Still true today (2026), safe superlative**: Voyager 2 remains the ONLY
  spacecraft ever to have flown past Uranus or Neptune. NASA's proposed
  Uranus Orbiter and Probe has no approved launch date, realistically 2030s+.

## 3. The Golden Record & Pale Blue Dot (CONFIRMED unless noted)

- Committee chaired by **Carl Sagan**; members Frank Drake, **Ann Druyan**
  (creative director), Linda Salzman Sagan (artist), Jon Lomberg (art
  director), Timothy Ferris (music producer).
- **Real, well-documented anecdote (not legend)**: Sagan and Druyan fell in
  love during the project. On June 3, 1977, two days after privately telling
  each other, Druyan recorded her own brainwaves/heartbeat for the record's
  data track "thinking about love and about Sagan" — days before she even
  learned NASA had approved the record.
- Contents: **115 images** (use this NASA figure, not 116); spoken greetings
  in **55 languages**, opening with 6,000-year-old Akkadian, closing with Wu
  Chinese; **~90 minutes of music**, 27 tracks — real confirmed highlights:
  Chuck Berry's "Johnny B. Goode," Blind Willie Johnson's "Dark Was the
  Night, Cold Was the Ground," plus Bach/Mozart/Beethoven/Stravinsky and
  folk tracks from Peru, New Guinea, Senegal.
- Physical object: 12-inch gold-plated copper disc + cartridge/stylus,
  aluminum jacket. Cover diagram (Sagan/Lomberg) shows playback instructions
  and a **pulsar map** (same diagram design used on the Pioneer plaques) —
  14 pulsars pinpointing Earth's location/epoch.
- "Message in a bottle" framing: Voyager 1 will pass within ~1.7 light-years
  of the star **AC+79 3888 (Gliese 445)** in roughly **40,000 years** — not
  because it's the nearest star now, but relative stellar motion. Sagan's own
  framing: the record is only ever "encountered and played" if advanced
  spacefaring civilizations exist in interstellar space — explicitly
  symbolic, not a working transmission.
- **Pale Blue Dot**: photographed **Feb 14, 1990, 05:22 GMT**, ~40 AU / 3.7
  billion miles out. Sagan pushed for it for **"eight years and six
  requests"** before the imaging team agreed (resistance was over risking
  camera optics near the Sun, and limited resources). Part of a 60-image
  "Family Portrait" mosaic (Neptune/Uranus/Saturn/Jupiter/Earth/Venus;
  Mercury/Mars/Pluto too faint or obscured to capture).
- **Sagan's exact quote (verbatim, safe to use)**: "Look again at that dot.
  That's here. That's home. That's us. On it everyone you love, everyone you
  know, everyone you ever heard of, every human being who ever was, lived out
  their lives... every saint and sinner in the history of our species lived
  there—on a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam." — *Pale Blue Dot: A Vision
  of the Human Future in Space* (1994).
- Real motion video exists of the **June 6, 1990 press conference** where
  Sagan/Ed Stone unveiled the photo (see footage plan below) — a genuine,
  usable real-video beat for this chapter.

## 4. Interstellar crossing, the 2024 glitch, and the live 2025-2026 power-down (CONFIRMED unless noted)

- **Heliopause crossing**: Voyager 1 **Aug 25, 2012** (announced Sept 2013),
  confirmed NOT visually but via the plasma wave instrument detecting a
  jump in electron density (~0.002 → ~0.08-0.1 cm⁻³), triggered by a solar
  eruption Voyager could "hear" as plasma oscillations. Voyager 2 crossed
  **Nov 5, 2018**, corroborated directly by its still-working plasma
  instrument plus 3 others.
- **The 2024 chip glitch (real, dated, with a real human quote)**: Nov 14,
  2023 — Voyager 1 began returning garbled telemetry for ~5 months. Traced
  to one corrupted Flight Data Subsystem chip (~3% of FDS memory, including
  live code) — cause unknown (worn chip or a cosmic-ray/energetic-particle
  strike). JPL couldn't fix the hardware remotely, so they rewrote the
  affected code, split it, and relocated it into unused memory, rewriting
  cross-references. First relocated code sent **April 18, 2024**, confirmed
  working **April 20, 2024**; full science data from all 4 active
  instruments resumed by **June 2024**. Real quote, Voyager project manager
  **Suzanne Dodd** (Live Science, early 2024): *"The people that built the
  spacecraft are not alive anymore."* She also described relying on paper
  documentation: "you do this archaeology dig to get documents." (Note: the
  NASA "Team Celebrates Engineering Data Return" image, PIA26275, is a
  STILL PHOTO of this moment, dated April 23 2024 — not motion video; see
  footage plan.)
- **Instrument shutdown timeline**: two instruments off per craft since
  2024; Voyager 1's cosmic ray subsystem off **Feb 25, 2025**; Voyager 2's
  low-energy charged particle (LECP) instrument off **Mar 24, 2025**;
  Voyager 1's own LECP off **April 17, 2026** (had run almost continuously
  since 1977 — 49 years — likely the real event behind the "oldest
  instrument" headlines, do not treat any "days ago" framing as a separate
  new event without a fresh primary source). Voyager 1 now down to just 2
  instruments (magnetometer + plasma wave subsystem).
- **The live, current, best-hook story: the "Big Bang" fix.** JPL developed
  a maneuver — internally nicknamed "the Big Bang" — that swaps groups of
  powered devices for lower-power alternatives while keeping the craft warm
  enough to keep functioning, without permanently killing a whole
  instrument. Executed on **Voyager 2 in July 2026**, confirmed successful
  in a **science.nasa.gov/blogs/voyager post dated Aug 4, 2026** — bought
  Voyager 2's three remaining instruments roughly one more year; without it,
  another instrument would have gone dark before end of 2026. The same test
  succeeded on **Voyager 1 in late July 2026** (mission manager Kareem
  Badaruddin: "this 'Big Bang' test has gone remarkably smoothly"), full
  rollout "in the coming months" — and NASA notes a real chance Voyager 1's
  LECP could even be switched back ON if it works. **This is the freshest,
  most defensible currency hook available — use it, not a vague "shutting
  down" framing.**
- **Projected end**: after the 2025-2026 shutoffs, at least one science
  instrument is projected to keep running into the 2030s on each craft; the
  bare engineering "I'm alive" signal could keep reaching the Deep Space
  Network until **around 2036** (NASA's own outermost stated boundary — use
  this instead of a flatter "2025-2030" estimate).
- **What survives after**: both craft, once silent, continue independent
  orbits around the center of the Milky Way (~225 million years per orbit)
  for billions of years — a real NASA-sourced framing ("will wander the
  Milky Way for billions of years"), until a possible disruption from the
  Andromeda-Milky Way collision in ~4 billion years.
