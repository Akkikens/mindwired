## 1. THE DEBRIS ASSESSMENT TEAM (DAT)

**CONFIRMED** (CAIB Vol I Ch.6, pp. 142, 151, 166) — Co-chairs verified exactly as the brief hypothesised:
- **Rodney Rocha** 🧍 — NASA's designated Chief Engineer for the Thermal Protection System; Division Chief Engineer, ES-Structural Engineering Division, JSC; Chair, Space Shuttle Loads & Dynamics Panel. CAIB calls him "the team's **NASA Co-Chair**."
- **Pam Madera** 🧍 — **United Space Alliance engineering manager**, the other Co-Chair. CAIB: "a Debris Assessment Team that would be **Co-Chaired by United Space Alliance engineering manager Pam Madera**." (p. 142)

**CONFIRMED — how it formed (Flight Day 2, Fri 17 Jan 2003):** The deputy manager of JSC Shuttle Engineering notified Rocha of the strike and approximate debris size. "It was Rocha's responsibility to coordinate NASA engineering resources and work with contract engineers at United Space Alliance, who together would form a Debris Assessment Team." The USA deputy manager of Shuttle Engineering classified the strike **"out-of-family"** — i.e. of greater concern than previous debris strikes. (p. 142)

**CONFIRMED — the organisational-limbo finding (the single best structural fact in this section).** CAIB sidebar, p. 142:
> "…the resultant group should, according to standing procedures, become a Mission Evaluation Room **Tiger Team**. Tiger Teams have clearly defined roles and responsibilities. Instead, the group of analysts came to be called a Debris Assessment Team. While they were the right group of engineers working the problem at the right time, **by not being classified as a Tiger Team, they did not fall under the Shuttle Program procedures** described in Tiger Team checklists, and as a result were **not 'owned' or led by Shuttle Program managers. This left the Debris Assessment Team in a kind of organizational limbo, with no guidance except the date by which Program managers expected to hear their results: January 24th.**"

### The Crater model
**CONFIRMED** (pp. 143–145, and the boxed sidebar "THE CRATER MODEL," p. 144):
- Crater is a **Boeing** empirical algorithm predicting penetration depth of debris into TPS tile. Its ancestor equation was developed **in 1966 during Apollo** to assess micrometeoroid "cratering"; modified 1979–1985 for Orbiter "acreage" tiles.
- Formula as printed: `p = 0.0195(L/d)^0.45 (d)(ρP)^0.27 (V-V*)^(2/3) / [(ST)^(1/4)(ρT)^(1/6)]`
- Validated only against **test projectiles of maximum 3 cubic inches**, fired at **single tiles, not groups of tiles as actually installed**. "No tests were performed with larger debris objects **because it was not believed such debris could ever impact the Orbiter**."
- **Why it was out of range — the exact numbers (CAIB table, p. 144):**

| Parameter | Validated test value | STS-107 analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | up to 3 cu. in. | 10" × 6" × 20" = **1,200 cu. in.** * |
| Length | up to 1 inch | ~20 inches * |
| Cylinder dims | ≤ 3/8" dia × 3" | 6" dia × 20" |
| Block dims | ≤ 3"×1"×1" | 6"×10"×20" * |
| Tile material | LI-900 acreage tile | LI-2200 * and LI-900 |
| Projectile shape | cylinder | block |
| d (diameter) | 0.4–2.0 in | **6 inches** * |
| V | up to 810 fps | ~700 fps |

(* = outside experimental/validated limits)

- **The multiplier — be precise here.** CAIB gives two figures and explains why: at the time, the DAT "believed that the debris was **640 times larger**"; the **Board's best estimate is that it was actually 400 times larger** than the calibration projectiles (pp. 143, 160). Use **"roughly 400 times larger than anything Crater had ever been validated against — the engineers at the time thought it was 640 times"**.
- **First-ever on-orbit use:** "The use of Crater to assess the damage caused by foam during the launch of STS-107 was **the first use of the model while a mission was on orbit**." (p. 143)
- **The operator:** "an engineer certified by Boeing to use Crater… had received formal training… but he had **only used the program twice before, and had reservations about using it** to model the piece of foam debris that struck Columbia." He did not consult Boeing's more experienced Huntington Beach engineers. **Boeing completed the transfer of Crater responsibility from Huntington Beach to Houston in January 2003 — STS-107 was the first mission the Huntington Beach engineers were not directly involved with.** (p. 145)

### What Crater actually predicted — DISPUTED IN POPULAR TELLING, CONFIRMED IN CAIB
**CONFIRMED (p. 145):** "For the Thermal Protection System tile, **Crater predicted damage deeper than the actual tile thickness.** This seemingly alarming result suggested that the debris that struck Columbia would have exposed the Orbiter's underlying aluminum airframe to extreme temperatures, **resulting in a possible burn-through during re-entry.**"

The DAT discounted this on two grounds: (1) calibration tests showed Crater over-predicts, so it was labelled "conservative"; (2) the Crater equation **does not model the tile's denser lower "densified" layer**.

**CONFIRMED — the separate RCC analysis, which is the part most tellings omit (p. 145):** A *different*, Crater-*like* algorithm, calibrated in 1984 against **ice** projectiles (0.875" dia × 3.75" long), was used for RCC. It "indicated that **impact angles greater than 15 degrees would result in RCC penetration.**" The transport analysis produced 15 candidate strike regions; **12 scenarios predicted a tile impact, exactly one predicted an RCC leading-edge impact at a 21-degree angle.** Because foam is less dense than ice, the DAT used "a **qualitative extrapolation** of the test data and engineering judgment to conclude that a foam impact angle up to 21 degrees would not penetrate the RCC. **Although some engineers were uncomfortable with this extrapolation, no other analyses were performed to assess RCC damage.**" CAIB: "The assumptions and uncertainty embedded in this analysis were **never fully presented** to the Mission Evaluation Room or the Mission Management Team."

## 2. THE IMAGING REQUESTS — THE HEART OF THE EPISODE

### ⚠ The single most important structural correction
The CAIB counts **three** discrete imagery requests, plus **eight** separate "missed opportunities." **All three requests originated on the NASA/contractor side. There was no request that "originated from the Department of Defense side."** What existed on the DoD side was (a) NASA people phoning DoD to ask *what it would take*, and (b) one **unsolicited DoD offer** of imagery support on 29 Jan (Missed Opportunity 8), which was accepted only on a "not-to-interfere" basis and never executed. Do not let the script imply a DoD-originated request.

### The three requests, verbatim from CAIB's own summary box (Vol I p. 166)
> **IMAGERY REQUESTS**
> 1. **Flight Day 2.** Bob Page, Chair, Intercenter Photo Working Group → Wayne Hale, Shuttle Program Manager for Launch Integration at Kennedy Space Center (**in person**).
> 2. **Flight Day 6.** Bob White, United Space Alliance manager → Lambert Austin, head of Space Shuttle Systems Integration at Johnson Space Center (**by phone**).
> 3. **Flight Day 6.** Rodney Rocha, Co-Chair of Debris Assessment Team → Paul Shack, Manager, Shuttle Engineering Office (**by e-mail**).

**All three CONFIRMED.** Detail on each:

**Request 1 — CONFIRMED (p. 140).** Fri 17 Jan, ~3:15 p.m. Bob Page 🧍 contacted Wayne Hale 🧍 (and Lambert Austin 🧍). Page's Photo Working Group had no usable image: of a dozen ground camera sites, **the first lost track of Columbia on ascent, the second was out of focus "because of an improperly maintained lens," and the third captured only the *upper* side of the left wing.** CAIB notes tartly: "**The Board notes that camera problems also hindered the Challenger investigation.**" Hale "holds a Top Secret clearance and was familiar with the process for requesting military imaging from his experience as a Mission Control Flight Director."

**Request 2 — CONFIRMED (p. 150).** Flight Day 6 (Tue 21 Jan). USA manager **Bob White** 🧍, "responding to concerns from his employees who were participating in the Debris Assessment Team," phoned **Lambert Austin** to ask what it would take to get on-orbit imagery. Austin then phoned the **DoD Manned Space Flight Support Office** — but "**Austin emphasized that this was merely information gathering, not a request for action.** This call indicates that Austin was unfamiliar with NASA/National Imagery and Mapping Agency imagery request procedures." DoD nonetheless started work. Lt Col Timothy Lee 🧍 to Don McCormack 🧍, 22 Jan 9:01 a.m.: *"FYI: Lambert Austin called me yesterday requesting DOD photo support for STS-107… to see if there is any FOD damage on the wing. **We are working his request.**"*

**Request 3 — CONFIRMED (p. 151).** After the DAT's first formal meeting on Flight Day 6, "**Each team member supported the idea to seek imagery from an outside source.**" Crucially, they **bypassed the normal chain** (MER → MMT → Flight Dynamics Officer) "largely due to a lack of participation by Mission Management Team and Mission Evaluation Room managers," and nominated Rocha to go up the *Engineering Directorate* instead. Rocha's e-mail to Paul Shack 🧍, **Tue 21 Jan 4:41 p.m.**, subject "STS-107 Wing Debris Impact, Request for Outside Photo-Imaging Help":
> "The meeting participants (Boeing, USA, NASA ES2 and ES3, KSC) **all agreed we will always have big uncertainties** in any transport/trajectory analyses and applicability/extrapolation of the old Arc-Jet test data **until we get definitive, better, clearer photos** of the wing and body underside. Without better images it will be very difficult to even bound the problem… Their answers may have a wide spread ranging from **acceptable to not-acceptable to horrible**, and no way to reduce uncertainty… **Can we petition (beg) for outside agency assistance?**"

CAIB's verdict on that routing (p. 152): "**Routing the request through the Engineering department led in part to it being viewed by Shuttle Program managers as a non-critical engineering desire rather than a critical operational need.**"

## 3. THE ROCHA "SAFETY POSTERS" E-MAIL — **HE DID NOT SEND IT**

**CONFIRMED — this is the most commonly mis-told detail in the entire episode, and the CAIB is unambiguous.** CAIB Vol I Ch. 6, p. 157:
> "After the meeting adjourned, Rocha read the 11:45 a.m. e-mail from Paul Shack, which said that the Orbiter Project was not requesting any outside imaging help. Rocha called Shack to ask if Shack's boss, Johnson Space Center engineering director Frank Benz, knew about the request. Rocha then sent several e-mails consisting of questions about the ongoing analyses and details on the Shuttle Program's cancellation of the imaging request. **An e-mail that he did not send but instead printed out and shared with a colleague follows.**"

**Exact text (CAIB Vol I p. 157), verbatim:**
> "In my humble technical opinion, this is the wrong (and bordering on irresponsible) answer from the SSP and Orbiter not to request additional imaging help from any outside source. I must emphasize (again) that severe enough damage (3 or 4 multiple tiles knocked out down to the densification layer) combined with the heating and resulting damage to the underlying structure at the most critical location (viz., MLG door/wheels/tires/hydraulics or the X1191 spar cap) could present potentially grave hazards. The engineering team will admit it might not achieve definitive high confidence answers without additional images, but, without action to request help to clarify the damage visually, we will guarantee it will not. Can we talk to Frank Benz before Friday's MMT? **Remember the NASA safety posters everywhere around stating, 'If it's not safe, say so'? Yes, it's that serious.**"

**Date: CONFIRMED as Wednesday 22 January 2003** (CAIB places it under "Flight Day Seven, Wednesday, January 22, 2003," after the 11:45 a.m. Shack e-mail and after the DAT's second meeting that afternoon). Independently corroborated by CBS News, which dates the draft to **Jan 22** (https://www.cbsnews.com/news/get-photos-begged-shuttle-engineer/).

**CONFIRMED — Rocha's own stated reason (CAIB p. 158):** "**When asked why he did not send this e-mail, Rocha replied that he did not want to jump the chain of command.** Having already raised the need to have the Orbiter imaged with Shack, he would defer to management's judgment on obtaining imagery."

**What Rocha DID send — do not conflate these:**
- 21 Jan 4:41 p.m., to Shack/Hamilton/Miller: the "petition (beg) for outside agency assistance" e-mail (Imagery Request 3). **SENT.**
- 22 Jan 11:19 a.m.: "…there are good scenarios (acceptable and minimal damage) to horrible ones… We do not know yet the exact extent or nature of the damage **without being provided better images**, and without such all the high powered analyses and assessments in work will retain significant uncertainties…" **SENT.**
- 26 Jan 7:45 p.m.: the post-hoc e-mail in which Rocha concurs with the "no safety of flight" conclusion for the MLG door case but flags a communications failure: "**the message of open work was not clearly given, in my opinion, to Linda Ham at the MMT. I believe we left her the impression that engineering assessments and cases were all finished**…" **SENT.** (p. 163)
- 19 Jan (FD4): Rocha e-mailed a JSC Engineering Directorate manager asking whether a Mission Action Request was in progress for the crew to visually inspect the left wing. "**Rocha never received an answer.**" **SENT, unanswered.** (p. 145 / Missed Opportunity 1)

## 4. WHAT THE CREW WAS AND WAS NOT TOLD

**CONFIRMED — the crew WAS told about the foam strike, on Flight Day 8, and they were told there was nothing to worry about.**

**CONFIRMED — why it was sent (CAIB Vol I p. 158):** "Mission Control personnel thought they should tell Commander Rick Husband and Pilot William McCool about the debris strike, **not because they thought that it was worthy of the crew's attention but because the crew might be asked about it in an upcoming media interview.**"

**The e-mail — Flight Director Steve Stich 🧍, Thursday 23 January 2003, 11:13 p.m., To: CDR; PLT, Subject: "INFO: Possible PAO Event Question" (CAIB Vol I p. 159), verbatim:**
> "Rick and Willie,
> You guys are doing a fantastic job staying on the timeline and accomplishing great science…
> There is one item that I would like to make you aware of for the upcoming PAO event on Blue FD 10… **This item is not even worth mentioning** other than wanting to make sure that you are not surprised by it in a question from a reporter.
> During ascent at approximately 80 seconds, photo analysis shows that some debris from the area of the -Y ET Bipod Attach Point came loose and subsequently impacted the orbiter left wing, in the area of transition from Chine to Main Wing, creating a shower of smaller particles. The impact appears to be totally on the lower surface and no particles are seen to traverse over the upper surface of the wing. **Experts have reviewed the high speed photography and there is no concern for RCC or tile damage. We have seen this same phenomenon on several other flights and there is absolutely no concern for entry.**
> That is all for now. It's a pleasure working with you every day."

**CONFIRMED (p. 159):** "This e-mail was followed by another to the crew **with an attachment of the video showing the debris impact. Husband acknowledged receipt of these messages.**"

**CONFIRMED — Engelauf's framing at the 24 Jan MMT (p. 161):** "I will say that crew did send down a note last night asking if anybody is talking about extension days… and we sent up to the crew about a 15 second video clip of the strike just so they are armed if they get any questions at the press conferences or that sort of thing, **but we made it very clear to them no, no concerns.**"

**So, precisely:** The crew knew a foam strike had occurred; they were shown video of it; and they were told in writing that there was "absolutely no concern for entry." They were **never told** that engineers wanted on-orbit imagery, that imagery had been requested and cancelled, or that the analysis rested on a model used 400× outside its validated range. **CAIB Report Synopsis, Vol I p. 9: "there were no indications to either the crew onboard Columbia or to engineers in Mission Control that the mission was in trouble as a result of a foam strike during ascent."**

**Related — Missed Opportunity 2, CONFIRMED (p. 148):** On Flight Day 1, Mission Specialist **David Brown** filmed External Tank separation on a Sony PD-100 camcorder and Payload Commander **Mike Anderson** photographed it with a Nikon F-5 / 400mm lens. Brown downlinked 35 seconds, in which the bipod ramp had rotated out of view. "**Based on how crews are trained to film External Tank separation, the Board concludes Brown did in fact have more film than the 35 seconds he downlinked.**" Nobody asked him for it.

## 5. RESCUE OR REPAIR — CAIB VOL II APPENDIX D.13

⚠ **This is the single most distorted item in the public record, in both directions. The following is what the study actually says.**

**Provenance — CONFIRMED (Vol II p. 391):** "During the course of the investigation, **the Board heard several NASA officials say there was nothing that could have been done to save Columbia's crew, even if they had known about the damage. The Board therefore directed NASA to determine whether that opinion was valid.**" The study was produced by the **NASA Accident Investigation Team, Houston, dated 22 May 2003**, and published **unedited** by the Board. Header caveat, verbatim:
> "**This is a NASA document and is published here as written, without editing by the Columbia Accident Investigation Board. The conclusions drawn in this report do not necessarily reflect the conclusions of the Board; when there is a conflict, the statements in Volume I… take precedence.**"

**The two CAIB-directed ground rules — CONFIRMED (Vol II p. 391, 396):**
- The scenarios assumed damage was **recognised early** and **"a decision to repair or rescue the Columbia crew would be made quickly, with no regard to risk."** CAIB: "**These ground rules were not necessarily 'real world,'** but allowed the analysis to proceed without regard to political or managerial considerations."
- **Assumption #1:** the damage is assumed to be a **6-inch-diameter hole in the lower surface of RCC panel #8** or a **10-inch missing T-seal between panels #8 and #9**, and assumed **visible and unambiguously catastrophic**. Verbatim: "**Whether these were the actual conditions on Columbia is not known.**" It "rules out damage consisting of a crack, an intact deformation of the panel, or damage to the attachment structure."
- **Assumption #2:** that NASA and the US government **would have been willing to launch Atlantis with no modification to the External Tank bipod ramp** — i.e. exposing a second crew to the very failure that had just occurred.

**The consumables number — CONFIRMED and precise (Vol II p. 395):** The limiting consumable was **lithium hydroxide** (CO₂ scrubbing), not oxygen. "the maximum on-orbit lifetime was **30 days total Mission Elapsed Time (MET), or until the morning of February 15.**" CAIB Vol I p. 173 adds that oxygen, the next most critical, "would require the crew to return on Flight Day 31." Flight surgeons judged **3.5% CO₂ (26.6 mmHg)** survivable long-term with "shortness of breath, fatigue, and headaches"; the crew would sleep more, stop all exercise and payload work, and **"the live animals in the SPACEHAB would be euthanized."**

**The rescue verdict — CONFIRMED, with all its qualifiers (Vol II pp. 395, 404–405; Vol I pp. 173–174):**
- Atlantis on Columbia's FD4 was in the OPF, **41 days from its scheduled 1 March launch**, main engines installed, SRBs already mated to its ET in the VAB.
- "By working **three around-the-clock shifts seven days a week**, Atlantis could be readied for launch, **with no necessary testing skipped**, by February 10." Vol II states launch on **10, 11 or 12 February** was possible; **"All three launch dates could have provided a rendezvous and EVA transfer of the crew prior to the depletion of consumables."**
- Crew of four (commander, pilot, two EVA astronauts); seven commanders, seven pilots, nine EVA-trained astronauts were available in January. Transfer by spacewalk, Columbia crew returning **four on the flight deck, seven in the mid-deck**; Atlantis weight 209,157 lb, c.g. 1081.2 in, **within certified limits**. Columbia would then be ground-commanded to a Pacific ditch (no ground capability to start APUs, deploy air data probes or extend gear).
- **CAIB Vol I p. 174 verdict, verbatim: "This rescue was considered challenging but feasible. To succeed, it required problem-free processing of Atlantis and a flawless launch countdown."**
- **The schedule compression table (Vol II p. 404) — the honest version of "feasible":** Orbiter processing 10 days → **7** (moderate, requires no failures); VAB flow 5 → **4** (moderate, no failures); pad flow, previous **record** 14 days → **11** (moderate, no failures); CDR/PLT training 48–54 weeks → **2 weeks** (moderate); **EVA crew training 40–50 weeks → 2 weeks (moderate to HIGH)**; COFR process 12–15 weeks → **2 weeks** (moderate).
- **Section 3.13 "Aggregate Risk," verbatim:** "**although each of the individual elements could be completed in a best-case scenario to allow a rescue mission to be attempted, the total risk of shortening training and preparation time is higher than the individual elements.**"
- **Section 3.15 "The Launch Decision," verbatim bullets:** "The mission would launch at night • The bipod foam problem was not well understood (what had changed?) • The flow required many activities to be done faster than normal, demonstrated templates • Several techniques would have their first use during the mission • **Risk to the second crew and vehicle must be considered fully** • **The timing of decisions and the information for their basis is critical and highly optimistic**."

**The repair verdict — CONFIRMED (Vol II pp. 395–396, 405–407; Vol I p. 173):**
- NASA rated the repair option **"high risk."** Vol I F6.4-1: "**The repair option, while logistically viable using existing materials onboard Columbia, relied on so many uncertainties that NASA rated this option 'high risk.'**"
- The actual plan, for a 6-inch RCC hole: an EVA crewmember stuffs a **stowage bag full of metal tools** into the wing cavity, then inserts **two or three Contingency Water Containers**, fills them from an airlock water hose, packs the remaining hole with **payload-bay-door insulation blankets**, and pins it with a **Teflon foot loop**; the wing is then "coldsoaked" to **freeze the water into ice**. The theory: insulation burns off fast, but ice + metal thermal mass may delay spar burn-through. (Vol I Fig. 6.4-1 shows astronauts hanging over the payload bay door reaching the wing **using a ladder scavenged from the crew module**.)
- For a missing T-seal: pack the gap with **tile fragments harvested from non-critical parts of Columbia**, shaped by the crew inside and pushed in on a second EVA. "**No testing has been done to determine how much friction is required to hold the tile in place or how large a gap between tiles would be acceptable.**"
- **Success criterion was NOT a safe landing.** Vol II §4.1, verbatim: "**Repair of the damage to the wing leading edge would be considered successful if spar burn through is delayed to allow the orbiter to reach an altitude in a sufficiently intact and controllable configuration to allow the crew to bail out.**" (Bailout at ~34,000 ft, combined with reduced vehicle weight, lowered perigee and increased angle of attack.)
- **Vol II executive summary, verbatim: "Limited thermal analyses of the repair and entry modification options were inconclusive, as there are too many unknowns concerning the flow path of the plasma and the resulting structural effects… therefore it is thought that the likelihood of success of this option would be low."**

**The bottom line, verbatim (Vol II p. 396):** "**The best option for the return of the crew was to attempt to transfer them to Atlantis. Both of these plans however, rely on the assumption that the RCC problem would have been found and be unambiguous, and that it would be acceptable to launch Atlantis with exposure to the same condition.**"

**CAIB Vol I Finding F6.4-2, verbatim (p. 174):** "**If Program managers were able to unequivocally determine before Flight Day Seven that there was potentially catastrophic damage to the left wing, accelerated processing of Atlantis might have provided a window in which Atlantis could rendezvous with Columbia before Columbia's limited consumables ran out.**"

And Vol I p. 174: "If Program managers had understood the threat that the bipod foam strike posed and were able to unequivocally determine before Flight Day Seven that there was potentially catastrophic damage to the left wing, these repair and rescue plans **would most likely have been developed, and a rescue would have been conceivable.**"

> **Note the two load-bearing qualifiers the internet always drops: "before Flight Day Seven," and "unequivocally."** Flight Day Seven is 22 January — the day the imagery request was cancelled. The rescue window closes on the same day the pictures were called off. That is the episode's structural irony, and it is fully documented.

- **The find that broke the case:** the **Modular Auxiliary Data System recorder** — **800 sensors on 9,400 feet of magnetic tape**, including left-wing leading-edge temperatures. It filled a 30-second telemetry gap and gave **14 extra seconds of data after LOS**.

## 7. THE CAIB'S CENTRAL FINDING — EXACT LANGUAGE

**CONFIRMED. CAIB Vol I, Executive Summary, p. 9, verbatim:**
> "**The organizational causes of this accident are rooted in the Space Shuttle Program's history and culture, including the original compromises that were required to gain approval for the Shuttle, subsequent years of resource constraints, fluctuating priorities, schedule pressures, mischaracterization of the Shuttle as operational rather than developmental, and lack of an agreed national vision for human space flight. # ⚠ DATA CORRECTIONS

Every item below is something widely repeated online, in documentaries, or in popular retellings that our research found to be wrong, exaggerated or imprecise. **Softened phrasing to use is given for each.**

### C1. "Linda Ham cancelled the imagery request." — IMPRECISE
**What's wrong:** The 8:30 a.m. phone call to USSTRATCOM was placed by "**the NASA Department of Defense liaison officer**," not by Ham. CAIB's construction is that Ham *terminated the actions*, and that Hale's DoD contact was "stopped per Linda Ham's orders."
**Use instead:** "The CAIB found that when Linda Ham terminated the actions the Department of Defense had already begun, she — in the Board's words — 'effectively terminated both the Intercenter Photo Working Group request and the Debris Assessment Team request.' The call to Cheyenne Mountain was made by NASA's Department of Defense liaison officer."

### C2. "Ham said 'I don't think there is much we can do' *about the damage*." — MISLEADING WITHOUT CONTEXT
**What's wrong:** The line is real but is routinely quoted stripped of its second half, which changes what it is a claim about.
**Use instead:** quote the whole sentence — "And I'm really I don't think there is much we can do so it's not really a factor during the flight because there is not much we can do about it. But what I'm really interested in is making sure our flight rationale to go was good" — and then give the CAIB's interpretation *as the CAIB's interpretation*, not as narration.

### C3. "Rodney Rocha sent the 'if it's not safe, say so' email and was ignored." — **WRONG. He did not send it.**
**What's wrong:** This is the most common error in the entire story. CAIB: "**An e-mail that he did not send but instead printed out and shared with a colleague follows.**"
**Use instead:** "Rocha wrote it on 22 January. He printed it out, showed it to a colleague, and never sent it. Asked why, he told the Board he did not want to jump the chain of command." **Do not** say "his warning went unanswered" of *this* email. His FD4 email asking whether the crew would inspect the wing is the one that genuinely "never received an answer."

### C4. "There was a Department of Defense request for imagery that NASA turned down." — WRONG AS STATED
**What's wrong:** All three CAIB-counted requests originated inside NASA/USA. What came *from* DoD was one **offer**, on 29 January (Missed Opportunity 8), conveyed via Mike Card to William Readdy.
**Use instead:** "The Department of Defense never asked NASA for anything — it offered. On 29 January, Associate Administrator for Space Flight William Readdy told NASA's safety office that the offer would be accepted, but, because the Mission Management Team had already concluded this was not a safety-of-flight issue, only on a low-priority 'not-to-interfere' basis. No imagery was ever taken."

### C5. "Spy satellites could have photographed the hole." — UNVERIFIED / OVERSTATED
**What's wrong:** No one has ever established what resolution would have been achievable. CAIB's actual finding cuts the other way — nobody in the decision chain was cleared to know.
**Use instead:** "The Board's finding is not that the satellites would have shown the hole. It is that **no one in the STS-107 chain of command held the clearance to know what national imaging assets could do**, and no one at NASA, United Space Alliance or Boeing tried to find out — so, in the Board's words, the Mission Management Team was 'making critical decisions about imagery capabilities based on little or no knowledge.'"

### C6. "Crater said the wing was fatally damaged and managers overruled it." — OVERSTATED
**What's wrong:** Crater predicted **tile** penetration deeper than tile thickness, implying possible burn-through; the DAT itself — not management — discounted that on two technical grounds (Crater's conservatism, the un-modelled densified layer). The RCC penetration warning (>15° angles) was likewise discounted by the *engineers*, via a qualitative extrapolation. The "no safety of flight" conclusion was the DAT's own.
**Use instead:** "Crater predicted penetration deeper than the tile itself. The engineers, not the managers, talked themselves out of it — they knew Crater ran conservative, and they knew it ignored the tile's hardened lower layer. What they could not do was check their reasoning, because to do that they needed a picture."

### C7. "The foam was 600 times bigger than Crater was built for." — IMPRECISE
**What's wrong:** Two different numbers exist and mean different things. **640×** is what the Debris Assessment Team believed at the time; **400×** is the Board's own best estimate of the truth.
**Use instead:** "About four hundred times larger than anything Crater had ever been tested against — and the engineers running it that week thought the figure was six hundred and forty."

### C8. "NASA could definitely have rescued the crew." / "There was nothing anyone could have done." — BOTH WRONG
**What's wrong:** The first drops D.13's ground rules ("no regard to risk"), its Assumption #2 (willingness to launch Atlantis into the same unfixed foam hazard), and §3.13's aggregate-risk caveat. The second is the exact claim the Board commissioned D.13 specifically to test — and it did not survive.
**Use instead:** "The Board heard NASA officials say nothing could have been done, so it ordered NASA to prove it. The answer, under ground rules that assumed the damage was spotted early and that risk was no object, was that a rescue by Atlantis was — in the Board's words — 'challenging but feasible,' requiring 'problem-free processing of Atlantis and a flawless launch countdown,' and requiring the United States to be willing to launch a second crew past the same unfixed bipod ramp. NASA's own study called the on-orbit repair option 'high risk' and rated its likelihood of success 'low.'"

### C9. "The repair plan would have got Columbia home." — WRONG
**What's wrong:** The repair option's own stated success criterion was not landing. It was surviving long enough to **bail out**.
**Use instead:** quote §4.1 verbatim: "Repair… would be considered successful if spar burn through is delayed to allow the orbiter to reach an altitude in a sufficiently intact and controllable configuration **to allow the crew to bail out**." Columbia was never coming home in that scenario.

### C10. "Appendix D.13 is the CAIB's conclusion." — WRONG ATTRIBUTION
**What's wrong:** D.13 is a **NASA** document, published unedited, with an explicit disclaimer.

## The SwRI foam test — CONFIRMED, CAIB Vol I §3.8 pp.79–83

**★ THE LINE THAT MAKES THIS THE THUMBNAIL — CAIB p.82 verbatim:** the tests
> "established that foam can breach the RCC, and also **counteracted the lingering denial or
> discounting of the analytic evidence.**"

The Board is saying the test's function was partly *institutional* — it existed to end an
argument, not only to answer a physics question. Context: on **5 Feb 2003** Program Manager
Ron Dittemore 🧍 said publicly *"It doesn't make sense to us that a piece of debris could be the

Earlier panel-6 test produced only a 5.5-inch crack; panel 8 was categorically different.

## Return to flight, and the recommendation that was cancelled
29 recommendations, **15 flagged [RTF]**. The Return to Flight Task Group (Stafford/Covey 🧍,
final report 17 Aug 2005) found NASA **met the intent of 12 of 15**; three it could not:
1. **R3.2-1 External Tank debris** — "the External Tank still sheds debris that could
   potentially cripple an Orbiter."
2. **R3.3-2 Orbiter hardening** — ⭐ verbatim: *"an early long-term plan for Orbiter hardening
   was abandoned after the National Policy decision to retire the Space Shuttle fleet no later
   than 2010."*
3. **R6.4-1** — met as to inspection, not as to on-orbit repair.

**STS-114, 26 July 2005: the tank shed foam again** (~0.9 lb off the PAL ramp). It **missed**
the orbiter. NASA grounded the fleet the next day. Next flight was 4 July 2006 — an 11-month
stand-down after a mission whose whole purpose was to prove the foam problem was fixed.

## 2. External Tank foam-shedding history

### 2.1 The design requirements that foam loss violated
CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.122, quoting NSTS-07700 "Flight and Ground System Specification – Book 1, Requirements":
> **3.2.1.2.14 Debris Prevention:** The Space Shuttle System, including the ground systems, shall be designed to preclude the shedding of ice and/or other debris from the Shuttle elements during prelaunch and flight operations that would jeopardize the flight crew, vehicle, mission success, or would adversely impact turnaround operations.

> **3.2.1.1.17 External Tank Debris Limits:** No debris shall emanate from the critical zone of the External Tank on the launch pad or during ascent except for such material which may result from normal thermal protection system recession due to ascent heating.

- The Thermal Protection System (tiles and RCC) was specified to withstand impacts of kinetic energy **less than 0.006 foot-pounds**. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.122.
- External Tank propellants stored at **−297 °F (LOX)** and **−423 °F (LH2)**; large areas machine-sprayed with one to two inches of foam, fixtures such as the bipod ramps **hand-sculpted with thicker coats**. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.121.
- "During flight, popcorn- to briefcase-size chunks detach from the External Tank." CONFIRMED (direct quote) — CAIB I, ch.6, p.121.

### 2.2 Scale of the problem
- "Damage caused by debris has occurred on every Space Shuttle flight, and most missions have had insulating foam shed during ascent." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.121.
- Photographic evidence of foam shedding exists for **65 of the 79 missions for which imagery is available**. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.122.
- Orbiters returned with an average of **143 divots** (upper + lower TPS tile surfaces), **31** averaging over an inch in one dimension; lower surfaces averaged **101 hits, 23** larger than an inch. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.122.
- Left bipod ramp (the "−Y ramp") foam loss confirmed by imagery on **7 of 113 missions**; but only **72** missions had imagery of sufficient quality — therefore **≈10 percent of imaged flights**. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.123.
- Of 66 flights with right-bipod imagery, foam loss from the right bipod was **never observed**. "NASA could not explain why only the left bipod experienced foam loss." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.123.
- Five of the seven left-bipod events occurred on Columbia; the Board concluded this is "probably the result of a bias in the sample," because before Challenger only Challenger and Columbia carried umbilical-well cameras. The bipod was imaged on 26 of Columbia's 28 missions vs Challenger 7/10, Discovery 14/30, Atlantis 14/26, Endeavour 12/19. CONFIRMED / the bias conclusion is ALLEGED (by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.6, p.124 (sidebar) and Finding F6.1-3, p.130.

**Event detail and the formal Program decision after each** (all CONFIRMED, CAIB I, ch.6, pp.123–126):

1. **STS-7 — 18 June 1983 (Challenger's 2nd flight).** Post-separation images showed a **19 × 12 inch** piece of left bipod ramp missing, ~25 shallow divots forward of the bipod struts and ~40 divots on the lower tank. **Decision:** the Program Requirements Control Board (PRCB) cited it as an **In-Flight Anomaly (IFA)**. It was closed at the next mission's Flight Readiness Review on the basis of **repairs to the Orbiter TPS** — the Board notes that "neither the Certificate of Flight Readiness documentation nor the Flight Readiness Review documentation referenced correcting the cause of the damage – the shedding of foam." (p.123)
2. **STS-32R — 9 January 1990 (Columbia's 9th flight).** Five intertank divots, **6 to 28 inches** in diameter, the largest extending into the left bipod ramp foam. Lower-surface inspection: **111 hits, 13** ≥1 inch. **Decision:** IFA assigned to the External Tank Project, closed at STS-36's FRR on the theory of **local voids** in the bipod ramp foam at the metal skin; NASA drilled small "vent holes" through the intertank foam. The Board notes NASA "is still studying this hypothesized mechanism of foam loss." (p.124)
3. **STS-50 — 25 June 1992 (Columbia).** An approximately **26 × 10 inch** piece separated from the left bipod ramp area. Post-mission inspection found a **9 × 4.5 × 0.5 inch divot in the tile — the largest area of tile damage in Shuttle history.** **Decision:** separate IFAs by the ET Project (MSFC) and the Integration Office (JSC). The Integration Office closed its IFA **two days before the next flight (STS-46)** by deeming TPS damage an **"accepted flight risk"**, recorded in **Integration Hazard Report 37**. (p.124)
4. **STS-52 — 22 October 1992 (Columbia).** An **8 × 4 inch** corner of the left bipod ramp lost, plus foam over the left jackpad. Post-mission: **290 hits** on upper and lower TPS tiles (higher than average), **16** >1 inch. **Decision: none at the time — the event was not detected until the Board directed NASA to review all available imagery after the accident.** No IFA was ever written. (p.124)
5. **STS-62 — 4 March 1994 (Columbia).** A **1 × 3 inch** piece missing from the rear face of the left bipod ramp, plus small pieces around the ramp. **Decision: none at the time — likewise undetected until the Board's post-accident imagery review.** (p.124) *(See ⚠ DATA CORRECTIONS re. the date.)*
6. **STS-112 — 7 October 2002 (Atlantis, ET-115)** — see §2.4 below.
7. **STS-107 — 16 January 2003 (Columbia)** — see §5. After the accident the PRCB **did** deem the foam loss an In-Flight Anomaly, assigned to the External Tank Project. (p.126)

### 2.4 STS-27R — Atlantis, 2 December 1988 (not a bipod event; the closest precedent for the debris strike itself)
All CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, pp.127–128; the Board's characterisations are ALLEGED (by the Board).

- **Date / vehicle:** Atlantis launched **2 December 1988** on STS-27R. "the largest debris event up to that time significantly damaged the Orbiter."
- **Timing:** Intercenter Photo Working Group analysis of tracking-camera imagery identified a large piece of debris that struck the TPS tile **at approximately 85 seconds into the flight**.
- **On-orbit response:** On **Flight Day Two**, Mission Control asked the crew to inspect Atlantis with a camera on the remote manipulator arm. Mission Commander R.L. "Hoot" Gibson 🧍 later stated that Atlantis "**looked like it had been blasted by a shotgun**" and, concerned the TPS had been breached, ordered the video transferred to Mission Control.
- **Damage found:** Post-mission inspections deemed it "**the most severe of any mission yet flown**." The Orbiter had **707 dings, 298 of which were greater than an inch** in one dimension. Damage concentrated outboard of a line right of the bipod attachment to the LOX umbilical line. **The debris knocked off a tile entirely, exposing the Orbiter's skin to re-entry heat.**
- **Near-miss:** structural damage was confined to the exposed cavity, which "happened to be at the location of a thick aluminum plate covering an L-band navigation antenna." Gibson 🧍 stated to the Board that, **were it not for the thick aluminum plate, a burn-through may have occurred.**
- **What the Program formally decided:** after post-flight inspection the **PRCB assigned In-Flight Anomalies to the Orbiter and Solid Rocket Booster Projects**. Marshall Sprayable Ablator (**MSA-1**) found embedded in an insulation blanket on the right OMS pod confirmed the ablator on the **right Solid Rocket Booster nose cap** as the most likely debris source. Because an improved ablator (**MSA-2**) would now be used on the SRB nose cap, **the issue was considered "closed" by the next mission's Flight Readiness Review.** "The Orbiter Thermal Protection System review team concurred with the use of the improved ablator without reservation."
- **The STS-27R team's own words**, quoted by the Board: "it is observed that program emphasis and attention to tile damage assessments varies with severity and that detailed records could be augmented to ease trend maintenance"; and "It is recommended that the program actively solicit design improvements directed toward eliminating debris sources or minimizing damage potential." (p.128; sources: "STS-27R OV-104 Orbiter TPS Damage Review Team Summary Report," Vol. I, February 1989, TM-100355, p.64; and "STS-27R National Space Transportation System Mission Report," NSTS-23370, February 1989)
- **The Board's comparison (ALLEGED, by the Board):** "The Board notes the distinctly different ways in which the STS-27R and STS-107 debris strike events were treated… Fourteen years later, when a debris strike was discovered on Flight Day Two of STS-107, Shuttle Program management declined to have the crew inspect the Orbiter for damage, declined to request on-orbit imaging, and ultimately discounted the possibility of a burn-through. In retrospect, the debris strike on STS-27R is a 'strong signal' of the threat debris posed… The Board views the failure to do so as an illustration of the lack of institutional memory in the Space Shuttle Program that supports the Board's claim… that NASA is not functioning as a learning organization." (p.127)

### 2.5 STS-112 — Atlantis, 7 October 2002 — **two missions before STS-107**
All CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, pp.124–126, 197; sequence STS-112 → STS-113 → STS-107.

- **Ascent event:** At **33 seconds after launch**, with Atlantis at **12,500 feet and Mach 0.75**, ground cameras observed an object leaving the External Tank which **impacted the Solid Rocket Booster / External Tank Attachment ring**. The debris then broke into multiple pieces that fell along the SRB exhaust plume. (p.124)
- **Damage to the SRB:** post-mission inspection confirmed damage to foam on the **forward face of the External Tank Attachment ring**; "The impact was approximately 4 inches wide and 3 inches deep." (p.124) Elsewhere the Board describes it as "a 4- by 3-inch gouge in the foam on one of Atlantis' Solid Rocket Boosters." (p.126)
- **Foam lost:** post-ET-separation crew photography showed a **4 × 5 × 12 inch (240 cubic-inch)** corner section of the left bipod ramp missing, exposing the super lightweight ablator on the bipod housing. (p.124)
- **No contemporaneous discussion:** "The post-launch review of photos and video identified these debris events, but the Mission Evaluation Room logs and Mission Management Team minutes do not reflect any discussions of them." (p.124)
- **What the Program formally decided — the pivotal decision:**
  - At the PRCB meeting after STS-112's return, the **Intercenter Photo Working Group recommended the bipod foam loss be classified an In-Flight Anomaly**.
  - In a meeting **chaired by Shuttle Program Manager Ron Dittemore 🧍** and attended by many managers later involved with STS-107, **including Linda Ham 🧍**, "the Program Requirements Control Board ultimately decided against such classification." Instead the PRCB Chairman assigned an **"action"** to the External Tank Project to determine root cause and propose corrective action. (p.125)
  - "This was inconsistent with previous practice, in which all other known bipod foam-shedding was designated as In-Flight Anomalies." (p.125)
  - The PRCB initially set **5 December 2002** as the report-back date, **even though STS-113 was scheduled to launch on 10 November 2002**. The due date then slipped until after the planned launch and return of STS-107. "The Space Shuttle Program decided to fly not one but two missions before resolving the STS-112 foam loss." (p.125)
- **STS-113 Flight Readiness Review, 31 October 2002** — attended by Dittemore 🧍 and Ham 🧍. Briefing charts presented by **Jerry Smelser 🧍**, Program Manager for the External Tank Project (NASA/MP31), stated (CONFIRMED as slide text, CAIB I, ch.6, p.125, Fig. 6.1-5):
  - Issue: "Foam was lost on the STS-112/ET-115 −Y bipod ramp (~ 4" X 5" X 12") exposing the bipod housing SLA closeout"
  - Background: "More than 100 External Tanks have flown with only 3 documented instances of significant foam loss on a bipod ramp"; "ET TPS Foam loss over the life of the Shuttle Program has never been a 'Safety of Flight' issue"
  - Rationale for Flight: "Current bipod ramp closeout has not been changed since STS-54 (ET-51)"; "There have been no design / process / equipment changes over the last 60 ETs (flights)"; "All ramp closeout work… was performed by experienced practitioners (all over 20 years experience each)"; "Ramp foam application involves craftmanship in the use of validated application processess" [sic]; "**Probability of loss of ramp TPS is no higher/no lower than previous flights**"; "**The ET is safe to fly with no new concerns (and no added risk)**"
- **The Board's assessment of that rationale (ALLEGED, by the Board — CAIB I, ch.6, pp.125–126):** "This rationale is seriously flawed. The first and third statements listed under 'Rationale for Flight' are incorrect… the bipod ramp design **had** changed, as of External Tank-76." And: "With no engineering analysis, Shuttle managers used past success as a justification for future flights."
- **The 99 percent figure:** the NASA Headquarters Safety Office presented a report estimating a **99 percent probability of foam not being shed from the same area**, even though no corrective action had been taken. The Board calls this "a sleight-of-hand effort to make the probability of bipod foam loss appear low," noting it equated left and right bipod loss, excluded the known STS-7, STS-32R and STS-50 losses by restricting the sample, and failed to project the rate across unimaged missions — a "'what you don't see won't hurt you' mentality." ALLEGED (by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.6, p.126.
- **Final classification:** FRR participants "ultimately agreed that foam shedding should be characterized as an **'accepted risk'** rather than a 'not a safety-of-flight' issue. Space Shuttle Program management accepted this rationale, and STS-113's Certificate of Flight Readiness was signed." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.126.
- **No hardware change:** "Despite the significant bipod foam loss on STS-112, Shuttle Program engineers made no External Tank configuration changes, no moves to reduce the risk of bipod ramp shedding or potential damage to the Orbiter on either of the next two flights, STS-113 and STS-107, and did not update Integrated Hazard Report 37." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.126.
- **Not raised before STS-107:** "the STS-107 Flight Readiness Review documents include no discussion of the still-unresolved STS-112 foam loss." Under Program rules this was not required because STS-112 had been logged only as an "action", not an IFA — but the Board states managers should have addressed it. CONFIRMED / the judgement ALLEGED (by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.6, p.126.
- **The Board's framing:** "Had the foam loss during STS-112 been classified as a more serious threat, managers might have responded differently when they heard about the foam strike on STS-107. Alternately, in the face of the increased risk, STS-107 might not have flown at all." ALLEGED (by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.6, p.125.
- Chapter 8 restates it as "a strong signal of danger 10 years after the last known bipod ramp foam event… After analysis, the biggest foam hit to date was redefined as a weak signal." ALLEGED (by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.8, p.197.

**Two further pre-STS-107 events worth naming on screen:**
- **STS-45, 24 March 1992 (Atlantis)** — post-mission inspection found **exposed substrate on the upper surface of right wing leading edge RCC panel 10**, from two gouges, one **1.9 × 1.6 inches** and one **0.4 × 1 inch**. The IFA was closed as "unexplained," but "most likely orbital debris." Later laboratory tests found man-made debris — stainless steel, aluminium and titanium — lodged in the RCC, but no source was concluded. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.129.
- **STS-87, 19 November 1997 (Columbia)** — significant loss from both intertank thrust panels; post-landing inspection found **308 hits, 244 on the lower surface, 109 larger than an inch**. The damage was classified **"out-of-family"** against 71 similarly configured flights. Root cause of earlier-than-normal "popcorning" traced to the switch from a **CFC-11 blowing agent to HCFC-141b**, beginning with ET-85 (STS-84) — a change made to reduce chlorofluorocarbon use. (Hand-sprayed foam, such as the bipod ramp, remained CFC-11.) The IFA stayed open across **nine** missions of corrective action (thinner thrust-panel foam, vent holes); after **STS-101**, the tenth mission, the PRCB concluded shedding had been reduced to an "acceptable level" and closed the IFA. The Board notes "these interventions merely reduced foam-shedding to previously experienced levels." CONFIRMED / final judgement ALLEGED (by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.6, p.129.

## 3. "In-family" / "out-of-family" and the normalisation of deviance

### 3.1 The formal definitions (CAIB I, ch.6, p.122, "DEFINITIONS" box) — CONFIRMED verbatim
> **In Family:** A reportable problem that was previously experienced, analyzed, and understood. Out of limits performance or discrepancies that have been previously experienced may be considered as in-family when specifically approved by the Space Shuttle Program or design project.

> **Out of Family:** Operation or performance outside the expected performance range for a given parameter or which has not previously been experienced.

> **Accepted Risk:** The threat associated with a specific circumstance is known and understood, cannot be completely eliminated, and the circumstance(s) producing that threat is considered unlikely to reoccur. Hence, the circumstance is fully known and is considered a tolerable threat to the conduct of a Shuttle mission.

> **No Safety-of-Flight-Issue:** The threat associated with a specific circumstance is known and understood and does not pose a threat to the crew and/or vehicle.

(Board's source notes: definitions 8 and 9 are drawn from NASA Program documentation; the Board also records that "Shuttle Program managers appear to have confused the notion of foam posing an 'accepted risk' with foam not being a 'safety-of-flight issue'." — CAIB I, ch.6, p.130.)

### 3.2 The Board's own language on normalisation — all ALLEGED (by the Board), quoted verbatim

**CAIB I, ch.6, p.130:**
> "The acceptance of events that are not supposed to happen has been described by sociologist Diane Vaughan 🧍 as the 'normalization of deviance.' The history of foam-problem decisions shows how NASA first began and then continued flying with foam losses, so that flying with these deviations from design specifications was viewed as normal and acceptable."

**CAIB I, ch.6, p.123:**
> "…what was originally considered a serious threat to the Orbiter came to be treated as 'in-family,' a reportable problem that was within the known experience base, was believed to be understood, and was not regarded as a safety-of-flight issue."

**CAIB I, ch.6, p.122:**
> "With each successful landing, it appears that NASA engineers and managers increasingly regarded the foam-shedding as inevitable, and as either unlikely to jeopardize safety or simply an acceptable risk… NASA and contractor personnel came to view foam strikes not as a safety of flight issue, but rather a simple maintenance, or 'turnaround' issue."

**CAIB I, ch.8, p.196 — section title: "Failures of Foresight: Two Decision Histories and the Normalization of Deviance":**
> "In both cases, engineers and managers conducting risk assessments continually normalized the technical deviations they found. In all official engineering analyses and launch recommendations prior to the accidents, evidence that the design was not performing as expected was reinterpreted as acceptable and non-deviant, which diminished perceptions of risk throughout the agency."

> "These anomalies were signals of potential danger, not something to be tolerated, but in both cases after the first incident the engineering analysis concluded that the design could tolerate the damage… For both O-rings and foam, that first decision was a turning point. It established a precedent for accepting, rather than eliminating, these technical deviations."

> "Engineers and managers incorporated worsening anomalies into the engineering experience base, **which functioned as an elastic waistband, expanding to hold larger deviations from the original design**. Anomalies that did not lead to catastrophic failure were treated as a source of valid engineering data that justified further flights."

> "Foam debris anomalies came to be categorized by the reassuring term 'in-family,' a formal classification indicating that new occurrences of an anomaly were within the engineering experience base. **'In-family' was a strange term indeed for a violation of system requirements.** Although 'in-family' was a designation introduced post-Challenger to separate problems by seriousness so that 'out-of-family' problems got more attention, by definition the problems that were shifted into the lesser 'in-family' category got less attention."

> "Engineers understood what was happening, but they never understood why. NASA continued to implement a series of small corrective actions, living with the problems until it was too late."

**CAIB I, ch.8, p.197 — weak vs strong signals:**
> "Some signals defined as weak at the time were, in retrospect, warnings of danger… because foam debris strikes were occurring frequently, like O-ring erosion in the years before Challenger, foam anomalies became routine signals – a normal part of Shuttle operations, not signals of danger."

> "On the October 2002 launch of STS-112, a large piece of bipod ramp foam hit and damaged the External Tank Attachment ring on the Solid Rocket Booster skirt, a strong signal of danger 10 years after the last known bipod ramp foam event… After analysis, the biggest foam hit to date was redefined as a weak signal."

**CAIB I, ch.7, p.181:**
> "Over the course of 22 years, foam strikes were normalized to the point where they were simply a 'maintenance' issue – a concern that did not threaten a mission's success."

**Feynman parallel** — the Board quotes Dr. Richard Feynman (d. 1988) of the Rogers Commission at length at CAIB I, ch.6, p.130, including: "The acceptance and success of these flights is taken as evidence of safety. But erosions and blow-by are not what the design expected. They are warnings that something is wrong… Erosion was not something from which safety can be inferred… If a reasonable launch schedule is to be maintained, engineering often cannot be done fast enough to keep up with the expectations of originally conservative certification criteria designed to guarantee a very safe vehicle. In these situations, subtly, and often with apparently logical arguments, the criteria are altered so that flights may still be certified in time. They therefore fly in a relatively unsafe condition, with a chance of failure of the order of a percent."

**Board's caution against the Russian-roulette framing** (useful counter-narration, CAIB I, ch.8, p.197): "that characterization is only possible in hindsight. It is not how NASA personnel perceived the risks as they were being assessed, one launch at a time."

### 3.3 Section 6.1 Findings (CAIB I, ch.6, p.130) — CONFIRMED verbatim
- **F6.1-1** "NASA has not followed its own rules and requirements on foam-shedding. Although the agency continuously worked on the foam-shedding problem, the debris impact requirements have not been met on any mission."
- **F6.1-2** "Foam-shedding, which had initially raised serious safety concerns, evolved into 'in-family' or 'no safety-of-flight' events or were deemed an 'accepted risk.'"
- **F6.1-3** "Five of the seven bipod ramp events occurred on missions flown by Columbia, a seemingly high number. This observation is likely due to [sampling bias from umbilical-well camera availability]."

### 3.4 Related quantified risk work
A 1990 study by **Dr. Elisabeth Paté-Cornell 🧍 and Paul Fishback 🧍** concluded the probability of losing an Orbiter on any given mission due to TPS tile failure was **approximately one in 1,000**; debris-related problems accounted for ~**40 percent** of that, tile debonding from other causes ~60 percent; ~**85 percent of the risk** was attributable to ~**15 percent of the "acreage"** tile. The Board states "NASA has not fully exploited the insights" of that work. CONFIRMED / final judgement ALLEGED (by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.6, pp.129–130.

## 4. The STS-107 mission profile and crew

### 4.1 Mission
- STS-107 was the **113th** Shuttle flight and Columbia's **28th**. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, p.27.
- A dedicated microgravity science mission: research spanning "life sciences, physical sciences, space and earth sciences, and education"; **more than 70 scientists** involved; research conducted by the **seven-member crew over 16 days**. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, p.27.
- Origin: designated a "research module" flight by the Flight Assignment Working Group at JSC in **June 1997**; in **March 1998** Administrator **Daniel Goldin 🧍** announced STS-107 would be a multi-disciplinary science mission modelled on STS-90 (Neurolab). Congress added **$15 million** (FY1999 conference report, Oct 1998) and later reserved **$40 million** for a second science mission, cancelled in **October 2002** with the money applied to STS-107. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, p.27.
- **Payload bay configuration:** SPACEHAB access tunnel; **SPACEHAB Research Double Module (RDM)**; the **FREESTAR** payload (Fast Reaction Experiments Enabling Science, Technology, Applications, and Research), mounted behind the RDM; the **Orbital Acceleration Research Experiment (OARE)**; and an **Extended Duration Orbiter pallet**. Additional experiments in the mid-deck and on the SPACEHAB roof. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, pp.27, 30.
- **Total liftoff payload weight: 24,536 pounds.** CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, p.30.
- FREESTAR replaced **Triana**, an Earth-observing satellite dropped after political disagreements between Congress and the White House. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, pp.27–28.
- **MEIDEX** (Mediterranean-Israeli Dust Experiment), a joint U.S./Israeli experiment, was added and "accompanied by an Israeli astronaut as part of an international cooperative effort." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, p.27.
- Crew announced by the Chief of the Astronaut Office in **July 2000**; requirements called for a crew of seven; **no** requirement for rendezvous, EVA, or use of the remote manipulator arm. The crew formed **Red and Blue teams** for around-the-clock operations. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, p.28.
- **13 delays over two years** (originally scheduled 11 January 2001). Three specific causes: removal of Triana; the Orbiter Maintenance Down Period running six months long (Kapton wiring), which pushed STS-109 (Hubble servicing) ahead of STS-107; and **Space Shuttle Main Engine propellant-system flowliner cracks** discovered ~one month before the planned 19 July 2002 launch date, which grounded the fleet for four months and caused STS-112 and STS-113 to be launched before STS-107. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, p.28.
- Training: **4,811 hours** mission training plus **~3,500 hours** payload-specific training. Ascent/Entry Flight Control Team began training with the crew **22 October 2002** (16 integrated ascent/entry simulations); Orbiter Flight Control Team began **23 April 2002**. **77** Flight Control Room operators on four shifts. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, p.30.
- Key pre-launch reviews: **Launch Readiness Review 18 December 2002** (KSC); final **Payload Safety Review Panel 8 January 2003**; **Flight Readiness Review 9 January 2003**. "No actions were assigned during the Flight Readiness Review. One exception was included in the Certificate of Flight Readiness pending the completion of testing on the Ball Strut Tie Rod Assembly." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.2, pp.30–31.
- Launched from **Launch Complex 39-A, 16 January 2003, 10:39 a.m. EST**. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, Executive Summary p.11 and ch.6 p.123.
- Landing was scheduled for **1 February 2003**. CONFIRMED — NASA astronaut biography (Chawla), https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/chawla_kalpana.pdf

## 6. Programme schedule pressure — the Node 2 milestone

All CONFIRMED as to facts; all judgements ALLEGED (by the Board).

- **The date: 19 February 2004** — the scheduled launch of **STS-120**, carrying **Node 2** (Italian-built, U.S.-owned) to the ISS, which would bring the Station to **"U.S. Core Complete"** and allow Europe and Japan to attach their laboratory modules. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.5, p.116; ch.6, p.131.
- Origin: after the ISS cost overruns, the Space Shuttle Program office concluded that **at a rate of four flights a year, Node 2 could be launched by February 19, 2004**. In testimony before the House Committee on Science on **7 November 2001**, ISS Management and Cost Evaluation Task Force Chairman **Thomas Young 🧍** identified what became known as a **"performance gate."** CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.5, p.117.
- The Task Force noted in its 2002 review that there was "significant risk with the Node 2 (February '04) schedule." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.5, p.117.
- **Effect on Columbia:** "To ensure adequate Shuttle availability for the February 2004 Node 2 launch date, Columbia would fly an International Space Station resupply mission" — although "Columbia was not optimal for Station flights – the Orbiter could not carry enough payload." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.5, p.117.
- "The White House and Congress had put the International Space Station Program, the Space Shuttle Program, and indeed NASA on probation." ALLEGED (by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.5, p.117.
- **Compression:** "With the Node 2 launch endpoint fixed, these delays caused the schedule to become ever more compressed. Meeting U.S. Core Complete by February 19, 2004, would require preparing and launching **10 flights in less than 16 months**." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.131.
- **The Board's question and its answer:** "The Board posed the question: Was there undue pressure to nail the Node 2 launch date to the February 19, 2004, signpost? … Various members of NASA upper management gave a definite 'no.' In contrast, the workforce [reported] considerable management focus on Node 2 and resulting pressure to hold firm to that launch date, and individuals were becoming concerned that safety might be compromised. **The weight of evidence supports the workforce view.**" ALLEGED (by the Board) / DISPUTED at the time by NASA upper management — CAIB I, ch.6, p.131.
- Employees attributed the date to Administrator **Sean O'Keefe 🧍**, appointed to execute an ISS management plan he had proposed as Deputy Director of the White House Office of Management and Budget. CONFIRMED (as employee attribution reported by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.6, p.131.
- **Visibility of the date:** the Shuttle and Station Program Managers briefed the Administrator monthly, and "the Node 2 schedule margin typically accounted for **more than half of the briefing slides**." NASA Headquarters mailed managers in the human spaceflight program a **screen saver counting down to February 19, 2004** (Fig. 6.2-3). CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, pp.132–133.
- **Link to STS-112/STS-113:** "Had the STS-113 mission been delayed beyond early December 2002, the Expedition 5 crew on board the Space Station would have exceeded its 180-day on-orbit limit, and the Node 2 launch date, a major management goal, would not be met." ALLEGED (by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.6, p.125. Restated at p.138: "The pressing need to launch STS-113 to retrieve the International Space Station Expedition 5 crew before they surpassed the 180-day limit and to continue the countdown to Node 2 were surely in the back of managers' minds during these reviews."
- **Comparison with pre-Challenger:** "There were four Shuttle launches to the Space Station scheduled in the five months from October 2003, through the launch of Node 2 in February 2004. To put this in perspective, the launch rate in 1985, for which NASA was criticized by the Rogers Commission, was nine flights in 12 months – and that was accomplished with four Orbiters and a manifest that was not complicated by Space Station assembly." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.136.
- Third shift of workers approved at Kennedy in late 2002; workforce required to work the 2002–03 winter holidays, and likely the 2003 winter holidays, to preserve margin. **By December 2002, every bit of padding in the schedule had disappeared**, and Program managers estimated Node 2 launch would be **one to two months late**. CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, pp.136, 138.
- **The Board's conclusion (verbatim, CAIB I, ch.6, p.139):**
  > "The agency's commitment to hold firm to a February 19, 2004, launch date for Node 2 influenced many of decisions in the months leading up to the launch of STS-107, and may well have subtly influenced the way managers handled the STS-112 foam strike and Columbia's as well."
  > "When a program agrees to spend less money or accelerate a schedule beyond what the engineers and program managers think is reasonable, a small amount of overall risk is added. These little pieces of risk add up until managers are no longer aware of the total program risk, and are, in fact, gambling. Little by little, NASA was accepting more and more risk in order to stay on schedule."
- **Findings F6.2-1 to F6.2-7 (CAIB I, ch.6, p.139), verbatim key items:**
  - F6.2-1 "NASA Headquarters' focus was on the Node 2 launch date, February 19, 2004."
  - F6.2-3 "The capabilities of the system were being stretched to the limit to support the schedule."
  - F6.2-4 "The four flights scheduled in the five months from October 2003, to February 2004, would have required a processing effort comparable to the effort immediately before the Challenger accident."
  - F6.2-5 "There was no schedule margin to accommodate unforeseen problems. When flights come in rapid succession, there is no assurance that anomalies on one flight will be identified and appropriately addressed before the next flight."
  - F6.2-6 "The environment of the countdown to Node 2 and the importance of maintaining the schedule may have begun to influence managers' decisions, including those made about the STS-112 foam strike."
- **Recommendation R6.2-1:** "Adopt and maintain a Shuttle flight schedule that is consistent with available resources. Although schedule deadlines are an important management tool, those deadlines must be regularly evaluated to ensure that any additional risk incurred to meet the schedule is recognized, understood, and acceptable." CONFIRMED — CAIB I, ch.6, p.139.
- **Chapter 7 framing:** "Delays for flowliner and Ball Strut Tie Rod Assembly problems left no margin in the schedule between February 2003 and the management-imposed February 2004 launch date for the International Space Station Node 2." ALLEGED (by the Board) — CAIB I, ch.7, p.198. Note the Board's phrase **"management-imposed."**

## ⚠ DATA CORRECTIONS

1. **"The foam struck the wing at 81.7 seconds."** — Imprecise; 81.7 s is the **separation** time, not the impact time.
   **Use instead:** "The foam separated from the External Tank's left bipod ramp at 81.7 seconds after launch and struck the left wing 0.2 seconds later, at 81.9 seconds." (CAIB I, Exec. Summary p.9; ch.2, p.34; ch.3, p.59.)

2. **"The foam was travelling at about 545 mph."** — True only as a *relative* velocity, and easy to misread as airspeed. The foam's absolute speed at impact was ~1,022 mph; the Orbiter was doing ~1,568 mph.
   **Use instead:** "The Orbiter overtook the decelerating foam at a closing speed of about 545 mph — roughly 800 feet per second. The Board's image-analysis range was 625 to 840 feet per second, and its formal finding states approximately 775 feet per second." (CAIB I, ch.3, pp.59–62.)

3. **"The foam weighed 1.67 pounds."** — This is the mass of the **test projectile the Board selected as most representative**, not a measurement of the actual debris, which was destroyed.
   **Use instead:** "The Board never recovered the foam. It settled on a reference projectile — 19 by 11.5 by 5.5 inches, 1.67 pounds, at 2.4 pounds per cubic foot — as the best representation of the piece that came off the bipod ramp, and fired that at RCC panel 8." (CAIB I, ch.3, pp.62, 81–82.)

4. **"The foam was about the size of a briefcase / it was 24 by 15 inches."** — Both the volume and the dimensions carry large stated ranges, and the Board revised them after the accident.
   **Use instead:** "The Board's post-accident best estimate was 1,200 cubic inches, within a range of 1,026 to 1,239 cubic inches. Dimensions were 21 to 27 inches long by 12 to 18 inches wide — the Board wrote this as 24 (±3) by 15 (±3) inches. Its thickness and shape were never determined from imagery; only that it was flat and relatively thin." (CAIB I, ch.3, pp.59–61, Fig. 3.4-5.)

5. **"The foam hit RCC panel 8."** — The Board's finding is more hedged than the Executive Summary's headline.
   **Use instead:** "Imagery narrowed the impact to less than 20 square feet across RCC panels 6 through 9. The Board's formal finding (F3.4-2) says 'in the vicinity of RCC panels 6 through 9 or the tiles directly behind.' Combining further imagery work, transport analysis and the recovered debris, the Board concluded that a smaller area in the immediate vicinity of panel 8 — specifically the lower half of panel 8 — was credible." (CAIB I, ch.3, pp.59–62; Exec. Summary p.9.)

6. **"STS-27 was in December 1988."** — The mission is designated **STS-27R** in NASA and CAIB documentation. STS-27 appears as a separate label on the Board's own Figure 6.1-6 axis. Say **"STS-27R"** when quoting the Board.

7. **"STS-62 launched in October 1994."** — The Board's own summary table (Fig. 6.1-7, p.128) prints **October 4, 1994**, which contradicts its Figure 6.1-1 (p.123, **03/04/94**) and its body text (p.124, **March 4, 1994**).
   **Use instead:** "March 4, 1994. Note that the CAIB report's own summary table misprints this date as October 4, 1994." (CAIB I, ch.6, pp.123, 124, 128.)

8. **"STS-112's foam gouged the SRB attach ring 4 inches by 3 inches."** — The report gives this two ways.
   **Use instead:** "The impact was approximately 4 inches wide and 3 inches deep in the foam on the forward face of the Solid Rocket Booster's External Tank Attach ring." (CAIB I, ch.6, p.124; the phrasing "a 4- by 3-inch gouge" at p.126 is the Board's shorthand for the same damage.)

9. **"STS-112 was the sixth bipod foam loss, and NASA knew it."** — At the time, NASA believed there had been only **three** such events. The STS-113 FRR slide says "only 3 documented instances." Events four and five (STS-52, STS-62) were **discovered by the Board after the accident**.
   **Use instead:** "STS-112 was the sixth left-bipod foam loss in hindsight. At the Flight Readiness Review, managers were told it was only the third." (CAIB I, ch.6, pp.124–125.)

10. **"NASA said there was a 99% chance the foam wouldn't come off again."** — Attribute precisely: this was a **NASA Headquarters Safety Office** report presented at the STS-113 FRR, and the Board rejected the arithmetic.
    **Use instead:** "The NASA Headquarters Safety Office presented a report estimating a 99 percent probability that foam would not be shed from the same area — even though no corrective action had been taken. The Board called that calculation 'a sleight-of-hand effort to make the probability of bipod foam loss appear low.'" (ALLEGED, by the Board — CAIB I, ch.6, p.126.)

11. **"Bipod foam came off on about 6% of flights (7 of 113)."** — The Board explicitly rejects this denominator.
    **Use instead:** "Foam loss from the left bipod area occurred on approximately 10 percent of flights — seven events out of the 72 missions for which imagery was good enough to tell." (CAIB I, ch.6, p.123; ch.8, p.196.)

12. **"Columbia was about 8,000 pounds heavier than the other orbiters."** — Specific weight deltas (e.g. 158,289 lb at rollout; ~7,900 lb heavier than Endeavour) are widely repeated from Wikipedia/GlobalSecurity and were **not** found in CAIB Volume I in this pass. **UNVERIFIED against a primary source.**
    **Use instead (fully sourced):** "Columbia had been manufactured to an early structural standard that resulted in the airframe being heavier than the later Orbiters. Coupled with a more-forward centre of gravity because of the internal airlock, Columbia could not carry as much payload weight into orbit as the other Orbiters — which made it less desirable for Space Station missions." (CAIB I, ch.2, p.28.) If a number is needed on screen, source it separately and caption it as non-CAIB.

13. **"Columbia was 22 years old."** — Correct as the Board phrases it, but anchor it to first flight rather than to construction: the airframe arrived at KSC in **March 1979**, first flew **12 April 1981**, and STS-107 launched **21 years and 9 months** after that first flight.
    **Use instead:** "The Orbiter that carried the STS-107 crew to orbit flew 22 years after its first flight" — the Board's own wording. (CAIB I, ch.1, pp.23, 25.)

14. **"Kalpana Chawla was an Indian astronaut."** — Imprecise. CAIB does not state her nationality; NASA's biography does.
    **Use instead:** "Kalpana Chawla, born in Karnal, India, came to the United States and became a naturalized U.S. citizen; she flew as a NASA astronaut on STS-87 and STS-107." (https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/chawla_kalpana.pdf) Ilan Ramon is the only non-American crew member: a Colonel in the Israeli Air Force and Israel's first astronaut. (CAIB I, ch.2, p.29.)

15. **"Laurel Clark was a Captain in the U.S. Navy."** — The Board records her as **"Commander (Captain-Select)"**. Use that exact form. (CAIB I, ch.2, p.29.)

16. **"The normalisation of deviance is the CAIB's term."** — It is not; the Board attributes it.
    **Use instead:** "The Board adopted a phrase from the sociologist Diane Vaughan 🧍 — 'the normalization of deviance' — to describe what it found." (CAIB I, ch.6, p.130.)

17. **"NASA classified the STS-112 foam loss as 'in-family'."** — Not the formal action taken. The formal decision was to **decline In-Flight Anomaly status and assign an 'action'** to the External Tank Project, and then at the STS-113 FRR to characterise foam shedding as an **"accepted risk"** rather than "not a safety-of-flight issue."
    **Use instead:** exactly that sequence. (CAIB I, ch.6, pp.125–126.)
