## BACKGROUND

- **[BG-1, CONFIRMED]** Deepwater Horizon was a **Reading & Bates Falcon-class
  semi-submersible Mobile Offshore Drilling Unit**, built 2001 by **Hyundai Heavy
  Industries** (South Korea), **owned by Transocean** (via subsidiary Triton Asset
  Leasing GmbH) and under a day-rate lease to **BP** since 2008. Source: National
  Commission report; USCG/BOEMRE JIT Vol. I; Wikipedia (corroborate against primary
  trade press before scripting as flat fact).
- **[BG-2, CONFIRMED, mileage approximate]** It was drilling BP's **Macondo Prospect
  exploratory well**, Mississippi Canyon Block 252, commonly cited as **~41 miles
  off the Louisiana coast** (some sources range 41–49 miles), in **~5,000 ft (1,525
  m)** of water, with a total well depth of **~18,360 ft** below the seafloor.
  Source: National Commission report; BP Accident Investigation Report.
- **[BG-3, CONFIRMED]** **126 people were aboard** April 20, 2010: 79 Transocean
  crew plus BP and contractor personnel, including Halliburton cementers. Source:
  USCG/BOEMRE JIT Vol. I.
- **[BG-4, CONFIRMED]** Three-company structure, central to the episode's "who's
  responsible" throughline: **BP** — well owner/operator, Macondo leaseholder, made
  final engineering/operational calls; **Transocean** — owned the rig, employed the
  drilling crew (driller, toolpusher, Offshore Installation Manager), operated/
  maintained rig equipment including the blowout preventer (BOP); **Halliburton** —
  contracted for cementing services (designed and pumped the final cement job) and
  mud-logging. This division of labor is why liability ultimately split three ways
  in court (see LG-7). Source: National Commission report; BP internal report.
- **[BG-5, CONFIRMED, figure approximate]** The rig cost BP roughly **$1 million per
  day** to lease and operate (ProPublica's widely-reported all-in figure); the bare
  Transocean lease day-rate alone is sometimes cited closer to $500,000/day —
  treat $1M/day as the commonly-used total-operating-cost figure for narration.
  Source: ProPublica.
- **[BG-6, CONFIRMED]** April 20 operations were a **"temporary abandonment"** —
  final cementing, a negative-pressure well-integrity test, and setting mechanical/
  cement plugs before evacuating the well to return later with a separate
  production rig — not a full completion. This context explains why a "test" and a
  "cement job," not full production, were the last acts before the blowout. Source:
  National Commission report; BP internal report.
- **[BG-7, CONFIRMED design choice / CONTEXT nuanced]** BP's engineering team chose
  a **"long string" casing design** — one continuous steel casing from reservoir
  depth to the wellhead — over a **liner + tieback** design. National Commission and
  BP's own report agree the long string was faster, cheaper, and had fewer
  mechanical seals (fewer long-term leak paths), but is harder to cement reliably in
  one continuous run at this depth. Source: National Academies "Macondo Well"
  report; National Commission report.
- **[BG-8, CONFIRMED sequence of events / UNVERIFIED precise internal chain]** April
  9, 2010: crew lost circulation ("lost returns"), forcing a design rethink. April
  14–15: BP engineers working with a Halliburton engineer ran cement-simulation
  software; early modeling suggested the long string could not be reliably cemented;
  the team considered switching to a liner, then proceeded with the long-string
  plan. **The exact final sequence of who overruled whom needs verification against
  the National Commission chief counsel's report or the House Energy & Commerce
  Committee's document index before scripting as a clean narrative** — secondary
  summaries sequence this slightly differently. Source: The Chemical Engineer;
  National Commission report.
- **[BG-9, CONFIRMED verbatim quotes — see Data Correction #6/#7]** BP drilling
  engineer **Brian Morel** emailed colleague Richard Miller on **April 14, 2010**
  (six days before the blast): *"this has been a nightmare well which has everyone
  all over the place."* Two days later (**April 16**), Morel raised the reduced-
  centralizer-count concern to BP operations drilling engineer **Brett Cocales**;
  Cocales replied: *"But, who cares, it's done, end of story, will probably be
  fine."* Both are real, documented emails released via the House Energy & Commerce
  Committee's document production, reported contemporaneously by CNN, CBS News, Fox
  News. Quote verbatim with attribution — do not paraphrase harsher or softer.
- **[BG-10, CONFIRMED existence of warning / DISPUTED adequacy of response]**
  Halliburton technical advisor **Jesse Gagliano** ran an OptiCem computer model two
  days before the blast showing "severe" gas-flow risk with fewer than 7
  centralizers and communicated this to BP. BP proceeded with only **6** centralizers
  instead of Halliburton's recommended **21**, to save ~10 hours. **DISPUTED**:
  whether BP's response adequately addressed the flagged risk — BP argued other
  measures compensated; National Commission and plaintiffs' experts argued they
  didn't. Source: National Commission testimony; NBC News.
- **[BG-11, CONFIRMED consensus / weighting DISPUTED]** All major investigations
  (National Commission, CSB, BP's own Bly Report) agree the **cement barrier at the
  bottom of the well failed to seal off hydrocarbons** — the physical root cause.
  Each report weights the *contributing* causes to that failure differently (see
  LG-narrative dispute below) — an important nuance for a "who's really to blame"
  documentary. Source: CSB Investigation Report Vol. 1.
- **[BG-12, CONFIRMED]** Halliburton admitted it **skipped a stability test** on the
  final cement slurry formulation actually used, after BP increased a key
  ingredient at the last minute; an earlier, different formulation had been tested
  and passed. Independent engineers hired by investigators later **could not
  reproduce a stable foam cement** in lab tests using the same materials/design.
  Source: New Civil Engineer; NPR; National Commission testimony.
- **[BG-13, CONFIRMED — no dollar figure, see Data Correction #9]** Schlumberger
  sent a specialized logging crew to the rig **April 18, 2010** to run a **cement
  bond log (CBL)** — the one direct test that could have verified whether the
  cement had actually sealed. On **April 20**, BP **canceled the test and sent the
  crew home**, reportedly to save the delay and associated cost. Source: NOLA.com
  (contemporaneous, May 2010); National Commission record.
- **[BG-14, CONFIRMED in direction, figures approximate]** By April 20, the well was
  significantly behind schedule and over budget — commonly cited as roughly **5–6
  weeks (35–45 days) behind** an original ~51-day plan, with tens of millions of
  dollars over an original ~$96 million budget (total cost by the blowout commonly
  cited around **$150 million+**). Treat precise dollar/day figures as approximate
  and attribute to source. Source: ProPublica; PMI case study.
- **[BG-15, ALLEGED-BUT-WELL-SOURCED — journalistic synthesis, not a single court
  finding in these exact terms]** ProPublica's investigative reporting, drawing on
  internal BP documents and the National Commission record, concluded BP made **a
  pattern of decisions that saved money and time** across well design (long string),
  cementing prep (centralizer count), testing (canceling the CBL), and finishing
  method (proceeding after the ambiguous negative test). Present in the doc as
  "investigators and reporters who reviewed BP's internal records identified a
  pattern of..." rather than as a bare fact — the underlying individual facts are
  each independently confirmed above.
- **[BG-16, DISPUTED — two directly conflicting sworn accounts]** Transocean's
  Offshore Installation Manager **Jimmy Harrell** testified under oath (May 27,
  2010, joint USCG/MMS hearing) that **he never felt pressure to speed up the job**
  and didn't recall a heated argument between managers over the well plan. This
  directly conflicts with Transocean chief mechanic **Doug Brown**'s separate sworn
  Senate Judiciary Committee testimony (also May 27, 2010) describing a heated
  midday argument on April 20 between "the company man" (BP) and Transocean crew
  over the seawater-displacement plan, including a remark: *"Well, I guess that's
  what we have those pinchers [the BOP] for."* Present both sworn accounts side by
  side, attributed by name; do not resolve the dispute in narration. Source: NBC
  News; NPR (both testimonies); C-SPAN.

## THE DISASTER

- **[DS-1, CONFIRMED, ±1–2 min sync tolerance across sources]** Timeline, April 20,
  2010: **~11:00 a.m.** — first negative-pressure test (via drill pipe) is run;
  result ambiguous/anomalous. **~5:00 p.m.** — BP well site leader **Don Vidrine**
  and the crew re-run the test; the drill pipe shows **~1,400 psi** where a
  successful test should show ~0 psi. Facing this, the crew — including senior
  toolpusher **Jason Anderson** — runs a **second test on the kill line**, which
  shows a "good," near-zero result. Believing the drill-pipe reading was a false
  artifact (the "bladder effect" — see below), Vidrine and the crew accept the test
  as successful. Source: National Commission report; BP internal report.
- **[DS-2, CONFIRMED — investigator finding, see Data Correction #8]** The National
  Commission found **no scientific evidence the "bladder effect" exists** — it was
  a fatal misinterpretation, not a real physical phenomenon. Source: National
  Commission report.
- **[DS-3, CONFIRMED]** **~8:00 p.m.** — crew begins displacing the heavy drilling
  mud in the riser with lighter seawater, the final step before setting cement
  plugs — this removes the primary pressure barrier (mud weight), leaving cement
  (already failed, unknown to the crew) as the sole barrier. Source: National
  Commission report.
- **[DS-4, CONFIRMED]** **9:08 p.m.** — displacement pumping is paused; a standpipe
  pressure reading of **~1,000 psi is recorded where ~0 psi was expected** —
  another warning sign. Source: National Commission report reconstruction.
- **[DS-5, CONFIRMED; exact "missed kick" duration UNVERIFIED]** **~9:30–9:40 p.m.**
  — crew members notice mud unexpectedly flowing onto the rig floor and mud-pit
  returns increasing — the physical sign of a **"kick"** (hydrocarbon influx). This
  is the first moment the crew recognizes an active well-control emergency. A
  commonly-cited "~40 minutes" figure for how long an earlier kick indication went
  unrecognized is **UNVERIFIED to a single primary-source page** — do not put a
  precise number on screen without confirming against the National Commission's
  minute-by-minute timeline chapter. Source: National Commission report; USCG/
  BOEMRE JIT.
- **[DS-6, CONFIRMED]** **9:41 p.m.** — the driller closes an annular blowout-
  preventer (BOP) seal attempting to shut in the well; it has **little to no
  effect** — the kick is already too far advanced. Source: National Commission
  report.
- **[DS-7, CONFIRMED]** **9:46–9:49 p.m.** — standpipe/drill-pipe pressure spikes to
  **~3,500 psi**; mud and then gas erupt onto the rig floor. The crew diverts flow
  toward the **mud-gas separator**, but its capacity is far exceeded — it vents a
  large gas cloud directly onto the rig deck instead of safely overboard. Source:
  National Commission report; BP internal report.
- **[DS-8, CONFIRMED]** **~9:49–9:50 p.m.** — the gas cloud reaches an ignition
  source; the **first explosion** occurs. Source: National Commission report; USCG
  JIT.
- **[DS-9, CONFIRMED]** **9:56 p.m.** — a second, larger explosion and resulting
  fire engulf the rig — the timestamp most consistently cited across the JIT report
  and data logs for when catastrophic fire took hold. Source: USCG JIT Vol. I;
  National Commission report.
- **[DS-10, CONFIRMED — root-cause consensus across all three primary reports]**
  The immediate technical cause was a **failed cement barrier** in the production
  casing, which allowed hydrocarbons to migrate up the wellbore undetected,
  compounded by: (1) failure to correctly interpret the negative-pressure test; (2)
  failure of the mud-gas separator to handle the gas volume; (3) failure of the BOP
  to seal the well on the emergency shut-in attempt. Source: National Commission
  report; USCG/BOEMRE JIT Vol. I & II; BP internal report.
- **[DS-11, CONFIRMED — but flag as a LATER, SEPARATE finding]** Within minutes of
  the explosions, crew members attempted to activate the BOP's emergency disconnect
  system (EDS) and the blind shear ram (BSR) — the last-resort device meant to cut
  the drill pipe and seal the well. The 2010–2011 reports' early public
  understanding was that the BSR never successfully engaged. **The US Chemical
  Safety Board's later Blowout Preventer Failure Analysis (DNV forensic work ~2011,
  CSB final 2014–2016) concluded the BSR likely DID activate that same night but
  failed to fully seal**, because the drill pipe had already buckled off-center
  under the surging pressure — the shear blades struck the pipe wall rather than
  cutting cleanly through it, worsening rather than stopping the flow. **Label this
  explicitly as the CSB/DNV finding** if used — it is distinct from, and postdates,
  the National Commission/USCG-BOEMRE/BP reports. Source: CSB Blowout Preventer
  Failure Analysis, 2014–2016.
- **[DS-12, CONFIRMED]** Crew evacuates by lifeboat, liferaft, and — for some —
  jumping directly into the Gulf from the burning rig, in some accounts with **less
  than five minutes' warning** once the fire alarm sounded. Most survivors are
  picked up by the standby supply vessel **Damon B. Bankston**; **17 injured** are
  evacuated by Coast Guard helicopter for medical treatment. Source: USCG JIT Vol.
  I; National Commission report.
- **[DS-13, CONFIRMED — exact toll, see Data Correction #1]** **11 crew members
  killed**: Jason Anderson (35, toolpusher), Aaron Dale "Bubba" Burkeen (37, crane
  operator), Donald Clark (49), Stephen Ray Curtis (40), Gordon Jones (28, drilling
  fluids/mud specialist), Roy Wyatt Kemp (27), Karl Kleppinger Jr. (38), Keith
  Blair Manuel (56), Dewey A. Revette (48, driller, 29 years with Transocean),
  Shane M. Roshto (22), Adam Weise (24). **115 of the 126 aboard evacuated safely;
  17 of those were injured.** Exact job titles for several of the 11 vary slightly
  by outlet — cross-check specific-role claims against the National Commission
  victim appendix before asserting a precise title on screen for any individual not
  listed above with a confirmed role. Source: USCG JIT Vol. I; National Commission
  report; The Advocate; NPR; AP wire "Short Portraits" (syndicated CSMonitor/NBC5/
  KRON4/Fox News).
- **[DS-14, CONFIRMED]** Fire burned **~36 hours**; the rig **sank April 22, 2010**
  around **10:21–10:22 a.m.**, roughly 5,000 ft to the Gulf floor, still attached
  to the wellhead via the riser, which then partially collapsed and began leaking
  from multiple points. **No dedicated real footage/photo of the sinking itself
  exists in any free/PD archive found** — see Archival Notes. Source: BP internal
  report; National Commission report; contemporaneous USCG reporting.
- **[DS-15, CONFIRMED]** The well flowed uncontrolled for **87 days** (April 20 –
  July 15, 2010). Multiple containment attempts (top hat, top kill, junk shot)
  failed through May–June 2010. Source: BP internal report; National Commission
  report.
- **[DS-16, CONFIRMED]** **July 15, 2010, ~2:25 p.m. CT** — a new capping stack
  successfully shuts in the well for the first time since the blowout; no more oil
  flows into the Gulf from that point, though the well is not yet permanently
  sealed. Source: NOAA Deepwater Horizon Oil Spill Timeline.
- **[DS-17, CONFIRMED]** **August 3–4, 2010** — BP performs the **"static kill"**
  (pumping heavy mud and cement through the capping stack), declared successful by
  BP and confirmed by the government.
- **[DS-18, CONFIRMED]** A relief well (begun early May 2010) intercepts the
  original Macondo wellbore **mid-September 2010**, and the well is **permanently
  sealed with cement** via a "bottom kill." The federal government officially
  declared the well **"effectively dead" on September 19, 2010**.
- **[DS-19, CONFIRMED, with a noted litigation dispute — see Data Correction #2]**
  The U.S. government's Flow Rate Technical Group figure, used in federal court
  findings, puts total discharge at **~4.9 million barrels** (~205 million
  gallons), of which roughly **810,000 barrels** were captured/recovered, leaving
  **~4.1 million barrels released into the Gulf** — the largest accidental marine
  oil spill in the history of the petroleum industry / the largest oil spill in US
  waters history. **DISPUTED (BP, in Clean Water Act civil litigation)**: BP's
  litigation experts argued for a materially lower total (as low as ~2.45–3.19
  million barrels released) — label any lower figure explicitly as "BP's
  litigation estimate," not a competing official-report finding. Source: National
  Commission report; NOAA timeline; U.S. government court filings.

**Where the reports genuinely diverge (the "who's really to blame" segment):**
- **BP's internal report (Sept 2010)** identifies eight specific technical/
  procedural failures and places the heaviest weight on **Halliburton's** cementing
  job and **Transocean's** BOP maintenance/crew reaction — it does **not** fault
  BP's own well design (long string) or its own centralizer-count decision as
  causal. **The National Commission's final report (Jan 2011)** explicitly rejects
  that framing, finding "systemic" failures spanning **all three companies**, with
  BP bearing ultimate regulatory responsibility as operator. **The Chief Counsel's
  Report (Feb 2011)** goes further, directly challenging BP's report's claim about
  why the surface cement plug was set so shallow. **Label every version of this
  dispute on screen by which report says it** — "BP's own report says... the
  National Commission's report says..." — never resolve it into a single flat
  verdict. Source: BP internal report (SEC filing); National Commission report;
  Bartlit Beck LLP summary of the Chief Counsel's Report.

## AFTERMATH & LEGAL OUTCOMES

- **[LG-1, CONFIRMED]** In **November 2012**, BP Exploration & Production Inc.
  agreed to plead guilty to **14 federal criminal counts**: 11 felony counts of
  seaman's manslaughter (one per victim), 1 felony count of obstruction of
  Congress, plus misdemeanor Clean Water Act and Migratory Bird Treaty Act
  violations. A federal judge (Sarah Vance) accepted the plea **January 29, 2013**.
  BP paid **$4 billion** in criminal fines/penalties — at the time, the largest
  criminal resolution in US history. **The 11 manslaughter counts map one-to-one to
  the 11 dead crew** — an accurate, DOJ-charging-document-sourced fact, not
  editorializing. Source: DOJ press release; NPR.
- **[LG-2, CONFIRMED]** Breakdown of the $4B criminal money: **~$1.256 billion** to
  a DOJ-administered fund; **~$2.394 billion** to the National Fish and Wildlife
  Foundation (Gulf barrier-island/river-diversion restoration); **$350 million** to
  the National Academy of Sciences (oil-spill-prevention/response research).
  Source: ELI plea agreement fact sheet.
- **[LG-3, CONFIRMED]** Separately, in **November 2012**, BP paid a **$525 million**
  penalty (third-largest in SEC history at the time) to settle SEC securities-fraud
  charges for materially understating the spill's flow rate to investors — BP
  publicly claimed ~5,000 barrels/day while internal data showed potential rates up
  to 146,000 bpd. Source: SEC press release.
- **[LG-4, CONFIRMED]** **July 2015** — BP reached an agreement in principle with
  the federal government and five Gulf states (AL, FL, LA, MS, TX) to resolve

## COLD-OPEN RECOMMENDATION

**Winner: Jason Anderson's last phone call.** *April 20, 2010. The Deepwater
Horizon drill floor, roughly 45–60 minutes before the well blows.* Senior toolpusher
**Jason Anderson**, 35, wasn't even supposed to be aboard — he'd finished his hitch
and was due at a new rig as senior toolpusher elsewhere, but stayed on because his
replacement hadn't shown up, using the extra time to clean out his locker and spend
more time with his crew. That night, he calls colleague **Randy Ezell**, off-shift
in his cabin, and tells him the negative-pressure test was a **"good test,"** with no
signs of trouble in the 30 minutes since. Ezell goes to bed around 9:30 p.m.
Anderson is on the drill floor when the well blows less than an hour later.

**Sourcing:** Ezell's sworn testimony at the 2013 federal civil trial (New Orleans,
Judge Carl Barbier) — [Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/BP-Transocean-Officials-Botched-Safety-Tests-Witness-Testifies-20130307),
corroborated by Bloomberg's parallel March 2013 trial coverage; the locker/
replacement detail and "not scheduled to be aboard" fact are AP wire ("Short
Portraits of 11 Who Died on the Deepwater Horizon"), syndicated identically across
CSMonitor, NBC5, KRON4, Fox News.

**Why it wins:** it is the single real, named, documented human decision-point in
the causal chain the format wants — a specific person, on the actual rig floor, in
the actual final hour, whose real, on-record words ("good test") are the exact
moment the fatal misread became irreversible. It fits the Fern cold-open formula
cleanly (a precise date, a named location, one small concrete action) and requires
zero invented dialogue — every line is something Anderson is on record as having
actually said or done. **Do not open on Gordon Jones's phone call** (a strong
candidate, but hold it for its proper place mid-episode, right before the blowout
sequence, as the emotional gut-punch beat rather than the hook) or on any of Mike
Williams's survivor quotes (save those as the verified first-person narration voice
carrying the audience through the explosion itself, not the opening scene).
