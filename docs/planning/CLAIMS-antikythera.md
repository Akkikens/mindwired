## ⚠ DATA CORRECTIONS & HONESTY RULES (baked in — do NOT revert)

1. **The April 2026 "50 artifacts / bronze throne armrest" story is RECYCLED
   2015.** dailygalaxy and similar aggregators reprint the **September 2015**

2. **⛔ The "heap of dead naked people" line is NOT a verbatim quote.** **Traced to its origin:** it appears in
   **Tony Freeth, *Scientific American*, 1 Jan 2022, with NO citation**, and in
   **Jo Marchant's *Decoding the Heavens* (2008)**, a narrative trade book that
   cites no primary source either. **The contemporary Athenian press (~440
   articles, Nov 1900 onward) contains no terror, no babbling and no corpses** —
   the contemporary version is flat: he signalled up, and he brought an arm.
   Price 1974, working from those newspapers, has no such line. The likeliest
   ancestor is Throckmorton (1970), written from mid-20th-c. interviews — and
   Throckmorton is **demonstrably unreliable here** (Jones catalogues him
   inventing details about Valerios Stais). Wikipedia gives a *different* unsourced wording ("a heap of
   rotting corpses and horses"). **Never put it in quotation marks or in narrator
   VO as Stadiatis's own words.** Narration-safe as reported tradition: *"The
   story that came down is that the diver surfaced babbling about a heap of dead
   bodies on the seabed — what he had actually seen were marble statues."* The
   corroded-marbles-look-like-corpses part is uncontested and safe.
   *(Third fabricated/unverifiable quote caught by a fan-out in two episodes —
   see CLAIMS-biosphere2.md §M.)*
3. **⚠⚠ THE STANDARD STORY IS WRONG — dimension 5 overturned dimension 1 on this.**
   Wikipedia, Price, and every popular retelling say **"Valerios Stais, 17 May
   1902."** Both halves are wrong:
   - **It was SPYRIDON Stais** — the *former* Minister of Education who had
     authorised the salvage and was by then **six months out of office**, visiting
     the museum with his wife and sister-in-law. Not an archaeologist: trained in
     maths and physics, taught school maths.
   - **The date is 18 or 20 May 1902 JULIAN = 31 May / 2 June 1902 GREGORIAN.**
     Price did not realise Greece was still on the Julian calendar, mis-assigned
     the weekday, and dated the Saturday a day early. That error propagated
     everywhere.
   - **Valerios Stais** (1857-1923) was the museum's **director**, Spyridon's
     **first cousin**, and **two years OLDER** — not, as Throckmorton wrote, a
     young nephew. He is not a villain: he ran the study of the finds for three
     years and published the standard 1905 monograph. He just didn't spot the gear.
     **On the record himself** (*To Asty*, 13 Dec 1902) he credited "the former
     minister, Mr. Stais."
   - Source: **Alexander Jones, "The Antikythera Mechanism 2," *Almagest* 7:1
     (2016), pp. 38-44** — reconstructed from ~440 contemporary Athenian press
     articles (Nikoli & Seiradakis, PoS 170:010).
   - **This is on-brand as an opening: the episode can begin by correcting the
     date and the name everyone else uses.**
4. **The mechanism was NOT recognised when recovered.** Fragments probably raised
   **20-24 July 1901**, reached Piraeus **3 August 1901**, and sat in the museum
   **roughly nine months** before anyone looked twice. **Never say the divers
   "found the world's first computer."**
5. **Depth is a RANGE, not a number.** 45 m (Wikipedia) / ~50 m (popular) / 55 m

6. **⛔ NEVER state a ship size or tonnage.** Estimates range from ~15 m to 50 m

7. **"Roman shipwreck" is loose.** It is a **Greek-built** merchantman with Greek

8. **The gearing is partly INFERRED.** Surviving fragments do not preserve the

## A. THE DISCOVERY, 1900

- **[CONFIRMED]** — Spring 1900 ("around Easter"), sponge divers from **Symi**
  captained by **Dimitrios Kontos**, en route to grounds off North Africa,
  sheltered from a storm off **Antikythera** (between Kythera and Crete) and hit
  a wreck off **Point Glyphadia**.
  - [Wikipedia — Antikythera wreck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antikythera_wreck) ·
    [WHOI](https://www.whoi.edu/ocean-learning-hub/ocean-topics/ocean-human-lives/underwater-archaeology/antikythera-shipwreck/)
- **[DISPUTED — 1900 vs 1901]** — Both are right about different things:
  **discovery 1900, principal recovery 1901.** Headlines saying "in 1901 sponge
  divers found…" compress the two.
- **[CONFIRMED]** — Kontos dived himself to verify and brought up a **bronze arm**
  from a statue.
- **[DISPUTED — the diver's name]** — English sources say **Elias / Ilias
  Stadiatis**. The project's own crew roster lists him as **"Ilias Lykopantis or
  Stadiatis," from Stadia in Asia Minor** — i.e. "Stadiatis" is a place-nickname
  ("the man from Stadia"), not a surname. **Worth a beat in the film.**
  - [Return to Antikythera — the sponge divers](https://antikythera.org.gr/history/1900-1901/sponge-divers/)

## B. THE SALVAGE, NOV 1900 – SEPT 1901

- **[CONFIRMED]** — **6 November 1900**: **Spyridon Stais**, Minister of
  Education, met the divers' representatives, offered a reward and secured a Navy
  transport; the troopship **Mykali** escorted them to the site. The operation ran
  **~ten months** — the first major underwater archaeological salvage in history,
  and **the divers themselves did the archaeology**.
  - [OUPblog — Alexander Jones, Oct 2017](https://blog.oup.com/2017/10/antikythera-shipwreck-finds/)
- **[CONFIRMED]** — **Standard diving dress**: copper helmet, canvas suit, lead
  boots, surface-supplied air from hand-cranked pumps. Bottom time ~**five
  minutes** per dive. **No decompression science existed** — Haldane's tables were
  published **1908, seven years too late.**
- **[CONFIRMED — the human cost]** — **One diver died of decompression sickness
  and two were left permanently paralysed**, ending work in summer 1901.
  - **[CONFIRMED — name]** The dead diver: **Georgios Kritikos** (roster:
    "Georgios Kritikos or Tourkokritikos," who "lost his life during the
    operations").
  - **[DISPUTED — origin]** Press call him a Symi diver; the project roster
    identifies him as **Cretan**. Say "a diver named Georgios Kritikos"; do not
    assign him to Symi on screen.
  - **⛔ [UNVERIFIED — date of death]** A circulating **28 April 1901** appears
    only in AI-generated aggregator text (Grokipedia and scrapes). **Do not put a
    date on his death.**
  - **⛔ [UNVERIFIED]** The two paralysed men are **not named** anywhere. **Do not
    invent names.**
- **[CONFIRMED]** — Other named crew: divers **Kyriakos Mountiadis** and
  **Georgios Mountiadis**, plus seven further divers and ten sailors.

## C. WHAT CAME UP — AND THE CENTRAL IRONY

- **[CONFIRMED]** — Raised: bronze and marble statuary, glassware, jewellery,
  coins, ceramics, ship fittings. Named pieces: the **Antikythera Youth/Ephebe**
  (bronze, c. 340 BC — finest Greek bronze of its period; first thing raised, as a
  detached arm in 1900); the **Antikythera Philosopher** (bronze head, glass
  eyes); the **Heracles of Antikythera** (marble, raised headless — **its head was
  not found until 2022**).
- **[DISPUTED — statue count]** — Wikipedia gives **36 marble sculptures** plus
  bronzes; other counts circulate. **Say "dozens of marble statues and the
  shattered remains of at least several bronzes."** A 2020 *Annual of the British
  School at Athens* paper argues the group may be a **gymnasion assemblage**
  rather than random luxury cargo.
- **🎯 [CONFIRMED — THE CENTRAL IRONY, and the beat the thumbnail pays off]** —
  The mechanism came up in **1901** as a corroded lump of bronze and wood and sat
  unexamined in the **National Archaeological Museum, Athens**. On **17 May
  1902**, archaeologist **Valerios Stais** noticed a **gear wheel embedded in it**
  and legible Greek inscriptions.
  - **Narration-safe:** *"The most important object ever pulled from the sea came
    up as a lump of rock. Nobody looked at it for a year. Then on the seventeenth
    of May, nineteen-oh-two, a museum archaeologist named Valerios Stais noticed
    something in the corrosion: a gear."*

## D. THE SHIP

- **[CONFIRMED]** — Wreck dated **c. 70-60 BC** (Foley's team works to c. 65 BC;
  Cousteau's 1976 coins minted **76-67 BC**). **Price's 1974 analysis dated the
  *mechanism* to c. 87 BC — an older object than the voyage**, which is itself a
  story beat. **[DISPUTED]** some sources give 60-50 BC.
- **[CONFIRMED]** — Cargo is multi-source Aegean/Eastern Mediterranean: Rhodian,
  Koan, Ephesian and (2025) **Chian** amphorae; **Parian** marble suggesting a
  Delos or Pergamon workshop; Syro-Palestinian or Egyptian glass. Destination
  Rome/Italy. Luxury goods, possibly **war spoils** (Sullan-era plunder).
  - **Narration-safe:** *"It was sailing west — out of the Greek east, toward Rome
    — loaded with the kind of cargo that only moves when someone rich, or someone
    victorious, is having it shipped home."*
- **🎯 [CONFIRMED — the 2025 hull find]** — The 2025 season recovered **three
  outer planks still joined to an internal frame** — the first articulated hull
  section ever raised. Spotted 2024, lifted intact 2025. **Confirms "shell-first"
  construction** (skin before skeleton), typical of the 4th-1st c. BCE
  Mediterranean. **Elm and oak**, ~0.40 × 0.70 m, planks **under 5 cm thick** —
  thinner than Cousteau's 1976 finds, raising open questions (upper hull? a
  repair? a smaller companion vessel?). Initial analysis suggests timber may date
  to **c. 235 BCE — the ship was already old when she sank.**
  - **Narration-safe:** *"In twenty twenty-five, for the first time, they brought
    up a piece of the ship itself — three planks still fixed to a rib. It proved
    she was built shell-first: the skin before the skeleton. And the wood may date
    to two hundred and thirty-five BC, which means she was already an old ship on
    the night she went down."*
  - [ESAG press release 09.07.2025 (PDF)](https://www.esag.swiss/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/25_CP_EN_ESAG_ANTIKYTHERA.pdf) ·
    [UNIGE](https://www.unige.ch/medias/en/2025/anticythere-une-portion-du-navire-antique-remonte-la-surface)

## E. LATER EXPEDITIONS

- **[CONFIRMED]** — **1953**: Cousteau surveys from *Calypso* with MIT's Harold
  Edgerton — **only three days**, a reconnaissance.
- **[CONFIRMED]** — **1976**: Cousteau returns for several weeks under archaeologist
  **Lazaros Kolonas**, filming *Diving for Roman Plunder*. Recovered **nearly 300
  objects** incl. the **coins that fixed the wreck's date**, hull planks, and
  **human skeletal remains** — plus the drinking vessel inscribed **"Pamphilos."**
- **[CONFIRMED]** — **"Return to Antikythera," 2012 onward**: co-directed by
  **Brendan Foley** 🧍 (WHOI) with **Theotokis Theodoulou** 🧍 and **Dimitris
  Kourkoumelis** 🧍. **Closed-circuit rebreathers with trimix gave up to three
  hours of bottom time where the 1900 divers had five minutes.**
- **[CONFIRMED]** — **2014 (15 Sept-7 Oct)**: experimental debut of the
  **Exosuit** (atmospheric hardsuit rated to ~300 m). Finds: a ~1.4 m lead anchor
  stock (~200 kg), a bronze **spear too heavy to be a weapon** (i.e. from a
  colossal statue), a *lagynos*. Team also sought a possible **second wreck** —
  the ship's travelling companion.
  - **⚠ HONESTY NOTE:** the Exosuit was the season's *press hook* and is reported
    as a **trial**, not the tool that produced the finds — those came from
    rebreather divers. **Do not imply the Exosuit did the excavating.**
- **[CONFIRMED]** — **2015 (Sept)**: 10-man team, **61 dives in 10 days** at 55 m,
  **50+ artefacts** incl. a bronze armrest possibly from a throne, a bone flute, a
  glass gaming pawn. Foley: *"This shipwreck is far from exhausted… it reveals how
  the '1 percent' lived in the time of Caesar."* **⚠ This is the story the April
  2026 posts recycle.**
- **[CONFIRMED]** — **2022 (23 May-15 June)**: relocated boulders **up to 8.5
  tonnes** covering the site. Found a **marble head of Farnese-type Hercules** —
  almost certainly the missing head of the headless Heracles raised in 1900-01 —
  and **two human teeth** with copper and wood traces. **Lorenz Baumer** 🧍 called
  the site *"the richest in the ancient world."*

## G. "PAMPHILOS" — THE 2016 SKELETON

- **[CONFIRMED]** — **31 August 2016**: partial skeleton in situ — two femurs,
  forearm bones, ribs, most of a skull **including both petrous bones** (the dense
  inner-ear bone that preserves DNA best). A **young man, probably mid-to-late
  twenties**. Foley 🧍 was reportedly three minutes into his first dive of the
  season. Described as the **first skeleton recovered from an ancient shipwreck
  since the advent of ancient-DNA analysis**.
- **🎯 [CONFIRMED — the nickname, a great beat]** — Nicknamed **"Pamphilos"**,
  Greek for *"friend to everyone"*, after **a drinking cup with that name etched
  on the rim, recovered by Cousteau in 1976**. A 1976 find named a 2016 skeleton
  from a 1st-century-BC wreck.
- **[CONFIRMED]** — **Hannes Schroeder** 🧍 (Natural History Museum of Denmark)
  confirmed the petrous bones and agreed to attempt extraction pending Greek
  permission. On camera: *"uncharted waters — I've never dealt with submerged
  remains like this before."*
- **🎯 ⚠ [UNVERIFIED — NO PUBLISHED RESULT. This is an ENDING, handled honestly.]**
  **No outcome of the Pamphilos DNA analysis has ever been published.** A 2020
  *Open Quaternary* review cites it only as **"Schroeder, in preparation."** Nine
  years on, nothing. **Do NOT say the DNA "revealed" anything; do NOT say it
  "failed."**
  - **Narration-safe:** *"They had the bones, and they had the one lab in the
    world that might read them. As of today, no result has ever been published."*
