## Origins — Swissair, the aircraft, the IFEN retrofit, the crew, the passengers

- **[CONFIRMED]** Swissair was nicknamed "the Flying Bank," reflecting a
  reputation (from roughly the 1950s-60s onward) for financial stability,

  Correction #1 on dating.
  — Wikipedia/Swissair; SimpleFlying; Institutional Investor/Wharton case
  studies (multiple independent secondary sources): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swissair
- **[CONFIRMED]** Swissair's fleet was grounded and SAirGroup collapsed
  into bankruptcy on/around October 2, 2001, three years after the crash.
  — SWI swissinfo.ch: https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/banking-fintech/national-legend_swissair-grounding-15-years-on/42481312
- **[CONFIRMED]** The primary driver of the 2001 collapse was the "Hunter
  Strategy" — aggressive equity stakes in weaker European airlines

## The Disaster

- **[CONFIRMED]** About 52-53 minutes after takeoff, cruising at 33,000
  ft, the crew detected an unusual odour in the cockpit at approximately
  21:10 EDT / 22:10 ADT (01:10:38 UTC) and began investigating.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, History of the Flight.
- **[CONFIRMED]** At 21:14 EDT / 22:14 ADT (01:14:15 UTC), the crew
  transmitted the first "Pan Pan Pan" call to Moncton Area Control Centre,
  reporting smoke in the cockpit and requesting diversion. Quoted (per CBS
  News's republished transcript, cross-checked against the TSB timestamp):
  "Swissair one-eleven heavy is declaring Pan Pan Pan. We have, uh, smoke
  in the cockpit, request deviate immediate right turn to a convenient
  place, I guess Boston." See Correction #8 on exact wording.
  — TSB Report A98H0003; CBS News, "The Last Conversation":
  https://www.cbsnews.com/news/the-last-conversation/
- **[CONFIRMED]** The crew initially requested Boston (roughly 234 nm
  away); Moncton Center proactively offered the closer Halifax (roughly 66
  nm at the time), which the crew accepted at 21:15 EDT / 22:15 ADT
  (01:15:06 UTC) — this was ATC's offer, not a crew miscalculation.
  — Wikipedia (direct citation of TSB/NTSB materials); code7700.com case
  study: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swissair_Flight_111
- **[CONFIRMED]** At approximately 21:24-21:25 EDT / 22:24-22:25 ADT
  (01:24:42-01:25:02 UTC) the crew stated "we now must fly manually"
  (autopilot lost) and then formally declared an emergency: "we are
  declaring emergency now, Swissair one eleven" — the flight's last
  transmission. Both the Flight Data Recorder and Cockpit Voice Recorder
  stopped about one minute later (01:25:40-41 UTC); last radar contact
  came at 01:26:04 UTC at 9,700 ft.
  — TSB Report A98H0003; CBS News transcript.
- **[CONFIRMED]** The fire most likely originated from electrical arcing
  above the cockpit ceiling (right side, near the rear wall), which
  ignited flammable cover material on MPET (metallized polyethylene
  terephthalate) thermal/acoustic insulation blankets; a segment of arced
  cable associated with the IFEN was found in that area. TSB explicitly
  could NOT conclude the arced IFEN cable alone was the fire's "lead
  event" — this precision matters; do not flatten it into "the
  entertainment system caused the fire" as a flat, proven fact.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Executive Summary:
  https://www.tsb.gc.ca/eng/medias-media/fiches-facts/A98H0003/sum_a98h0003.html
- **[CONFIRMED]** MPET-covered insulation blankets met the flammability
  certification standard in force when the MD-11 was certified (14 CFR
  25.853(b), Amendment 25-61), but TSB found those certification standards
  themselves were inadequate — they permitted a material that could
  ignite and sustain/propagate fire. The material's use was not illegal;
  the certification test itself was flawed — a more precise indictment
  than "they used a banned material."
  — TSB Recommendation A99-07: https://www.tsb.gc.ca/eng/recommandations-recommendations/aviation/1999/rec-a9907.html
- **[CONFIRMED]** As the fire spread, other flammable cabin materials
  became involved (silicone elastomeric end caps, hook-and-loop fasteners,
  foams, adhesives, insulation splicing tapes, MPVF cover material); a
  rapid succession of aircraft systems failures began roughly 13 minutes
  after odour detection; primary flight displays failed, forcing the crew
  onto small standby instruments.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Executive Summary.
- **[CONFIRMED — this directly answers the "fuel dump controversy"]** The
  crew's decision to have the cabin readied for landing and to dump fuel
  was consistent with them being unaware a fire was underway. TSB's own
  theoretical descent-profile analysis found that, given the fire's rate
  of progression, even an immediate approach beginning at the moment of
  the Pan Pan call would not have achieved a safe landing at Halifax
  regardless. See Correction #4 on citation precision. Public/media
  discourse has repeatedly framed the fuel-dump decision as a costly
  delay [DISPUTED, general aviation-enthusiast commentary, not TSB] — TSB's
  own finding is the direct rebuttal; script as myth-vs-finding ("many
  have wondered... TSB modeled it and found it wouldn't have mattered"),
  never as the actual cause of death.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Executive Summary.
- **[CONFIRMED]** The aircraft struck the Atlantic Ocean approximately 5
  nautical miles southwest of Peggy's Cove, Nova Scotia (approx.
  44°24'33"N 63°58'25"W) at approximately 21:31 EDT / 22:31 ADT (01:31:18
  UTC), roughly six minutes after the emergency declaration. The TSB
  report states plainly: "The aircraft was destroyed by impact forces.
  There were no survivors." All 229 aboard (215 passengers, 14 crew — 13
  Swissair plus 1 Delta Air Lines crew member repositioning) were killed.
  Debris field lies at ~55m (180 ft) depth.
  — TSB full investigation report A98H0003 (primary text):
  https://www.bst-tsb.gc.ca/eng/rapports-reports/aviation/1998/a98h0003/a98h0003.html
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Specific impact parameters (~300 knots groundspeed,
  ~20° nose-down pitch, ~110° bank angle, ~350 g impact force) are
  consistently repeated across secondary/wiki-mirror sources but not
  independently pulled from the primary TSB text this pass. See
  Correction #5 — do not display these as a hard on-screen stat without a
  primary citation.
- **[CONFIRMED — state once, factually, no forensic detail]** Given the
  impact conditions, death for everyone aboard would have been effectively
  instantaneous. See Correction #12 — never dramatize the underlying
  forensic recovery detail.
  — CMAJ/PubMed, forensic-pathology account of the identification effort
  (peer-reviewed, consistent with TSB's "destroyed by impact forces"
  finding): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9951448/

## The Official TSB Investigation

- **[CONFIRMED]** The recovery operation ("Operation Persistence," the
  Canadian Armed Forces' response) involved roughly 2,400 CAF personnel
  (navy, army, air force), about 450 RCMP officers (Operation Homage), and
  Canadian Coast Guard personnel; key vessels included HMCS Preserver
  (command ship), HMCS Moncton, HMCS Ville de Quebec, and the submarine
  HMCS Okanagan, which helped locate the flight recorders. The Flight Data
  Recorder was recovered September 6, 1998; the Cockpit Voice Recorder
  September 11, 1998; recovery was completed by December 1999, retrieving
  about 98% of the aircraft by weight (more than 126,000-127,000 kg of
  airframe plus over 18,000 kg of cargo) from ~55m of water.
  — Veterans Affairs Canada, official government history of Operation
  Persistence: https://www.veterans.gc.ca/en/remembrance/military-history/service-canada/operation-persistence
- **[CONFIRMED]** The U.S. Navy's USS Grapple (ARS-53), with divers from
  Mobile Diving Salvage Unit Two, assisted the Canadian-led recovery at the
  crash site; official DoD-dated imagery documents the ship and MDSU2
  divers operating there on September 12-13, 1998, using sonar/debris-
  survey equipment. (An "arrived Sept 9 / departed Sept 15" arrival window
  is UNVERIFIED beyond the confirmed Sept 12-13 DVIDS-dated presence — a
  primary DoD document confirming exact arrival/departure dates was not
  independently pulled this pass.)
  — DVIDS, official U.S. Navy photo caption, VIRIN 980913-D-D0439-7157:
  https://www.dvidshub.net/image/9208670/us-navy-divers-prepared-dive-crash-site-swissair-flight-111
- **[CONFIRMED]** Salvaged wreckage was brought to CFB Shearwater, where
  more than 350 investigators and aviation experts from around the world
  reconstructed a full-scale mock-up of the forward fuselage section to
  study the fire's origin and propagation.
  — The Canadian Encyclopedia / contemporaneous investigation reporting.
- **[CONFIRMED — the single most important claim in the episode]** TSB's
  final report (released March 27, 2003, after a 4.5-year, ~CA$57 million
  investigation — at the time the most expensive in Canadian aviation
  history) found the fire's likely origin was electrical arcing above the
  cockpit ceiling igniting flammable MPET insulation cover material, with
  an arced IFEN-associated cable segment found in the likely origin area —
  but TSB could not conclusively establish that wire alone as the fire's
  definitive "lead event." The report issued 23 total safety
  recommendations (14 during the investigation, 9 in the final report):
  aggressive in-flight firefighting procedures, tougher wiring
  flammability testing, backup power for flight recorders, and more. TSB
  also found the aircraft's smoke checklists did not adequately direct the
  crew to aggressively locate/eliminate the smoke source or expedite
  emergency-landing preparation, and recommended standby instruments be
  grouped and positioned in the crew's normal line of sight like primary
  flight instruments.

### Electrical failure sequence — detailed cascade & technical mechanism

- **[CONFIRMED]** The IFEN's power draw exceeded what the cabin AC bus
  could supply, so installers moved most of its power source to 115V AC
  Bus 2, a main engine-generator bus unaffected by the cockpit CABIN BUS
  switch.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Sections 1.18.7.5 / 2.14.1: https://tsb.gc.ca/sites/default/files/rapports-reports/aviation/a98h0003/eng/a98h0003.pdf
- **[UNVERIFIED — see Correction #16]** That AC Bus 2 was specifically "a
  higher-capacity bus meant for things like hydraulic pumps." No TSB
  support found anywhere in the 429-page report; AC Bus 2 is one of three
  main generator buses feeding sub-buses generally. Do not script this
  framing.
- **[DISPUTED]** That IFEN power was consolidated onto "a single heavy
  cable." There was one main 8-AWG feeder to a single 15A breaker, but from
  there power split via jumpers to FOUR separate 15A-breakered PSU cables —
  a branched, individually-breakered architecture, not one cable.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Section 1.6.11.2 (same URL as above).
- **[DISPUTED — see Correction #16]** That higher fault energy on Bus 2
  overwhelmed time-delayed breakers, explaining why they didn't trip. TSB's
  actual, explicit finding (Section 2.13.1) is that the arcs' electrical
  characteristics fell outside conventional breakers' time-vs-current trip
  curve — a generic problem with the aircraft's breaker technology
  (hence TSB's push for Arc Fault Circuit Breakers), not a bus-capacity- or
  bus-identity-specific one, and it would apply identically to a cabin-bus
  short.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Section 2.13.1 (same URL as above); corroborated
  by Lectromec: https://lectromec.com/swissair-111-incident/
- **[CONFIRMED]** 24 seconds after the CABIN BUS switch went OFF (0123:45
  UTC), the autopilot disconnected with a warning tone (0124:09 UTC).
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Table 1 / Section 2.18 (same URL as above).
- **[CONFIRMED]** Two yaw-damper channel failures followed: lower yaw
  damper 1A at 0124:54 UTC (channel 1B stayed on, indicating fire damage,
  not crew action) and upper yaw damper A at 0125:34 UTC.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Sections 1.18.8.22 / Appendix D (same URL as
  above).
- **[DISPUTED]** "Flight control computer channels" (plural) failed. TSB
  documents only ONE failure in this window: FCC-1 Channel A lost primary
  power at 0124:57 UTC, with FCC-1 data to the FDR stopping within 15
  seconds. Script as singular.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Section 1.18.8.11 (same URL as above).
- **[CONFIRMED]** The FDR began losing/shedding parameters (pressure
  altitude, computed airspeed, total air temperature) from 0125:06-07 UTC.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Sections 1.18.8.13 / 2.18 (same URL as above).
- **[CONFIRMED]** The left emergency AC bus failed at 0125:06 UTC, killing
  the captain's ADC-1 air data, DU1/DU3 displays, transponder Mode C, and
  pitot heat. The first officer's three displays going blank was a
  SEPARATE event ten seconds later (0125:16 UTC) on the right emergency AC
  bus, which TSB says "was still powered" — a different mechanism, not one
  bus-wide loss. Don't conflate the two.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Sections 1.18.8.12-1.18.8.17 (same URL as above).
- **[DISPUTED — see Correction #16]** That transponder, TCAS, VHF-1, and
  ACARS failed in that order before the CVR distorted and both recorders
  stopped. Transponder/TCAS/VHF-1 degradation and CVR distortion did
  precede the 0125:41 UTC recorder stoppage as claimed — but ACARS's actual
  unit failure is timestamped 0126:01 UTC, TWENTY SECONDS AFTER both
  recorders had already stopped. Reorder ACARS to AFTER the recorder
  stoppage in any script.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Sections 1.18.8.2.3 / 1.18.8.13 / 1.18.8.16 /
  1.18.8.18-20 and Appendix D (same URL as above).
- **[DISPUTED — see Correction #16]** That the full cascade took "about
  one minute" from the cabin-bus switch-off. TSB's own Table 1: switch OFF
  at 0123:45 UTC, both recorders stop at 0125:41 UTC — 1 minute 56 seconds,
  not about one minute. (The 24-second figure above is accurate only for
  the autopilot-disconnect sub-step, not the full cascade.)
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Table 1 (same URL as above).
- **[CONFIRMED]** Selecting the CABIN BUS switch OFF shut down recirculation
  fans, reversing attic airflow from aft-toward-fans to forward-toward-the-
  cockpit, drawing an ALREADY-BURNING fire's combustion by-products forward
  into the cockpit attic and down into the avionics bay — a genuine,
  explicit TSB finding, not an embellishment. TSB separately and explicitly
  rejects the idea that the switch itself electrically caused the
  failures (ruled out via the transponder briefly resuming 9 seconds after
  the recorders stopped, which required a bus that would have been dead
  had the switch caused the stoppage). Script the correct causal chain:
  switch-off → fans off → airflow reversal → accelerates a pre-existing
  fire → that fire's own arcing produces the cascade — not "the switch
  caused/triggered the failures."
  — TSB Report A98H0003, Sections 2.17.4.8 / 2.18 (same URL as above).
- **[CONFIRMED — see Correction #16]** Takeoff weight was over 530,000 lb
  (241,147 kg) against a maximum OVERWEIGHT EMERGENCY landing weight of
  about 480,000 lb (218.4 tonnes) — an emergency-only allowance above the
  normal certified max landing weight of ~440,000 lb (199.58 tonnes). Don't
  conflate the two landing-weight figures.
  — TSB A98H0003 Supporting Technical Information: https://www.tsb.gc.ca/eng/rapports-reports/aviation/1998/a98h0003/dit-sti/dit-sti-45.html
- **[DISPUTED — see Correction #16]** That the MD-11 dumps fuel at
  roughly 2,000-3,000 lb/min. TSB's own technical page states the maximum
  dump rate is 2,600 kg/min (~5,200 lb/min on main-tank pumps alone, ~6,000
  lb/min with auxiliary pumps running) — roughly double the commonly
  repeated figure.
  — TSB A98H0003 Supporting Technical Information — Fuel Dumping:
  https://www.tsb.gc.ca/eng/rapports-reports/aviation/1998/a98h0003/dit-sti/dit-sti-18.html
- **[CONFIRMED — elaborates Correction #4]** TSB's own descent-profile
  modeling found that even beginning descent immediately at the Pan Pan
  call (22:14 ADT) with a straight-in approach, the earliest possible
  Halifax arrival was approximately 22:27 ADT — and given the fire's
  progression, a safe landing likely would not have been achieved even on
  this earliest-possible profile. The exact TSB page/paragraph citation
  still has not been pulled — see Correction #4, this does not close that
  gap.
  — Multiple independent secondary summaries of TSB's finding.

## Third Pass — Arc Fault Circuit Breaker Legacy & Wiring Systemic-Risk Narrative (2026-08-24)

A third research pass, focused specifically on the direct engineering legacy
of the Section 2.13.1 finding, full-text-searched the primary 429-page TSB
report PDF (not just secondary summaries) plus all nine of TSB's final
numbered recommendations (A03-01 through A03-09) and the most relevant
interim recommendation (A01-03). This closes several gaps left open in the
first two passes and adds one major new sourced narrative: the direct
textual link, in the FAA's own regulatory language, between TWA 800 and
Swissair 111.

### What TSB's own report actually says about AFCB technology

- **[CONFIRMED — primary text, Section 1.18.5.2 "Circuit Breaker Design"]**
  The report states plainly, in its factual-background section (not just
  analysis): "The USN, the FAA, and aircraft manufacturers are sponsoring
  initiatives to address this shortfall in CPD technology. The goal is to
  develop an arc fault circuit breaker device appropriate for aircraft
  use." This means the AFCB development effort was already underway,
  jointly by the U.S. Navy, FAA, and manufacturers, by the time TSB wrote
  its report — it is accurate to say TSB's investigation ran *alongside
  and documented* an existing effort, not that the investigation alone
  spawned it from nothing. See Correction #16, which already establishes
  the trip-curve mechanism; this refines it with the report's own words.
  — TSB Report A98H0003, primary PDF text, Section 1.18.5.2:
  https://tsb.gc.ca/sites/default/files/rapports-reports/aviation/a98h0003/eng/a98h0003.pdf
- **[CONFIRMED — primary text, Section 2.13.1 "Circuit Breaker Technology,"
  full exact wording]** "Industry and government are presently engaged in
  various research and development efforts aimed at designing a CB that
  will detect and react to the full range of known arc fault events...
  The resulting Arc Fault Circuit Breaker (AFCB) is being designed to
  surpass the protection provided by traditional thermal CBs... Theoretically,
  had an AFCB been available to protect the IFEN PSU cables, the initial
  arcing events on exhibits 1-3790 and 1-3791 would have been detected by
  the AFCB, and the device would have tripped and de-energized the cable."
  This is TSB's own, direct, on-the-record statement that an AFCB
  theoretically would have caught the specific arcing event that started
  the SR 111 fire — the strongest, most citable line for the episode's
  engineering-legacy beat.
  — Same primary source, Section 2.13.1.
- **[CONFIRMED — an important, deliberately-caveated finding; do not flatten
  into "AFCBs would have prevented the crash"]** The same paragraph
  immediately qualifies itself: "While the new circuit protection devices
  will mitigate the wire damage from successive arcing events on the same
  wire, ignition of surrounding flammable material may still occur from
  the initial arcing event." TSB's own position is that an AFCB would have
  stopped the arc from recurring/escalating on that wire — NOT that it
  would have guaranteed no ignition at all, since the first spark itself
  can still ignite nearby flammable material before any breaker (thermal or
  arc-fault) reacts. Script as "TSB itself said this device likely would
  have caught the fault — but even TSB stopped short of saying it would
  have stopped the fire outright," a more honest and more interesting line
  than a flat "this technology would have saved the plane."
  — Same primary source, Section 2.13.1.
- **[CONFIRMED — TSB's own second-guessing of the technology's
  certification adequacy, Safety Action Section 4.3.7 "Arc-Fault Circuit
  Breaker Certification," full exact wording]** "While the AFCB trip
  characteristics will provide major improvements over the traditional
  aircraft CB design, these devices will not be certified to a standard
  that will require that the AFCB trips prior to the ignition of nearby
  flammable material. The Board is concerned that unless this aspect of
  the design specifications is addressed, AFCBs certified for use on
  aircraft will be capable of remaining energized long enough to ignite
  nearby flammable material." As of the March 2003 final report, TSB was
  on the record saying the very AFCB certification standards then being
  drafted (modeled partly on the residential Underwriters Laboratories UL
  1699 arc-fault-interrupter standard, dated 26 February 1999, per the
  report's own footnote) did NOT yet require an AFCB to trip fast enough
  to prevent ignition — only fast enough to stop repeat arcing on the same
  wire. This is a genuine, sourced complication to any "problem solved"
  framing.
  — Same primary source, Section 4.3.7 (page 296 in the printed report).

### The direct, sourced TWA 800 ↔ Swissair 111 wiring-narrative connection

This is the strongest new finding of this pass: a real regulatory document,
not analyst commentary, draws the connection explicitly.

- **[CONFIRMED — primary regulatory source, exact language]** The FAA's
  final rule "Enhanced Airworthiness Program for Airplane Systems/Fuel
  Tank Safety" (EAPAS/FTS), Docket No. FAA-2004-18379, published in the
  Federal Register on 8 November 2007 (72 FR 63403), effective 10 December
  2007 — which created new 14 CFR Part 26 ("Continued Airworthiness and
  Safety Improvements for Transport Category Airplanes") and amended Parts

  in 1996 involving a 747 airplane. Ignition of flammable vapors in the

  the area where the fire most likely originated." This is a real, sourced

### AFCBs today — NOT a blanket mandate; be precise about "standard/required"

- **[CONFIRMED]** As of this research pass, arc fault circuit breakers are
  **not** a blanket requirement on transport-category aircraft. 14 CFR
  25.1717 ("Circuit protective devices: EWIS") imposes a
  PERFORMANCE-based standard — wiring and circuit protection must be
  designed together "so that a fire or smoke hazard cannot be created
  under temporary or continuous fault conditions" — and permits multiple
  means of compliance (conventional thermal circuit breakers, fuses, or
  AFCBs), rather than mandating AFCB hardware specifically. Do not script
  "arc fault circuit breakers are now required on every airliner" — the
  accurate claim is narrower: they are one accepted, standardized,
  increasingly used compliance option, not a universal hardware mandate.
  — 14 CFR 25.1717 (eCFR); cross-checked against Lectromec's plain-English
  summary of the same regulation: https://lectromec.com/cfr-25-1717-circuit-protection-and-ewis/
- **[CONFIRMED — the clearest EXISTING AFCB use-case, and it traces more
  directly to TWA 800's mechanism than Swissair 111's]** FAA Advisory
  Circular 25.981-1D ("Fuel Tank Ignition Source Prevention Guidelines,"
  in the AC 25.981 lineage that runs through the TWA 800-driven SFAR
  88/fuel-tank-flammability rulemaking) names AFCBs (alongside ground
  fault interrupters) as an accepted means of providing fail-safe
  protection specifically for fuel-pump circuits, where service experience
  showed conventional thermal breakers "have not precluded arcing through
  metallic barriers into fuel tanks." This is the most concrete, named,
  current regulatory use of AFCB technology found this pass — and it is
  fuel-tank-ignition-focused (TWA 800's causal mechanism), not
  cabin/attic-wiring-fire-focused (Swissair 111's causal mechanism). Keep
  this distinction precise on screen: don't credit Swissair 111 alone for
  a rule whose most concrete AFCB application addresses a different
  accident's failure mode.
  — FAA AC 25.981-1D search-result summaries (direct fetch blocked by the
  document host this pass — verify the AC's own text before an on-screen
  citation card).
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Whether AFCBs are installed as standard/default
  equipment on current-generation widebodies (e.g., Boeing 787, Airbus
  A350). No source found this pass confirms or denies this either way —
  do not assert either "yes, they're standard now" or "no, they still

  — Lectromec, "Testing the Performance of AFCBs": https://lectromec.com/testing-the-performance-of-afcbs/

## Supplementary Research — Dr. Jonathan Mann's Real Significance (Third Pass, 2026-08-24)

A third research pass, focused specifically on verifying and expanding Dr.

### The "founder of health and human rights as a field" framing — CONFIRMED, not just a channel talking point

- **[CONFIRMED]** Mann is widely and specifically credited — by his own
  institution (Harvard), by peer-reviewed public health journals, and by
  law-school scholarship — as the founder of the modern health-and-human-
  rights field, not merely "a doctor who cared about human rights." Harvard's
  FXB Center for Health and Human Rights (which Mann founded in 1993 as the
  Bagnoud Center, serving as its first director) states this framing on its
  own institutional history page. A peer-reviewed retrospective is
  literally titled "Jonathan Mann: Founder of the Health and Human Rights
  Movement."
  — Harvard FXB Center, "First Director": https://fxb.harvard.edu/founding-director/
  ; PubMed/peer-reviewed retrospective (title as stated): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17018812/
  ; corroborating PDF: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6775887_Jonathan_Mann_Founder_of_the_Health_and_Human_Rights_Movement
- **[CONFIRMED]** The intellectual core of Mann's framework, developed
  through his work on HIV/AIDS in Zaire and at WHO: public health, ethics,
  and human rights are not separate concerns but complementary fields
  united by the same underlying goal of human well-being; social
  marginalization, discrimination, and stigmatization are themselves root
  causes of disease spread, not incidental to it; and governments that
  violate citizens' rights and dignity directly undermine the health of
  their populations. This reframed AIDS policy worldwide away from a purely
  clinical/virological problem and toward one where legal and social
  discrimination against at-risk groups was itself a public health
  variable — a genuinely novel, field-defining move credited specifically
  to Mann, not a general sentiment already common in public health before
  him.
  — Harvard FXB Center (same URL as above); Georgetown Law scholarship
  repository, Lawrence O. Gostin, "Public Health, Ethics, and Human Rights:
  A Tribute to the Late Jonathan Mann": https://scholarship.law.georgetown.edu/facpub/1817/
- **[CONFIRMED]** Mann co-founded and, with colleagues Sofia Gruskin and
  Daniel Tarantola, helped launch the peer-reviewed academic journal
  *Health and Human Rights: An International Journal* in 1994 — the
  field's first dedicated scholarly journal, evidence the "field" framing
  is institutional/academic, not just a biographical characterization
  after his death.
  — Harvard FXB Center, "First Director" page (same URL as above).
- **[CONFIRMED]** Mann's specific career arc supporting the "founder"
  claim: CDC Epidemic Intelligence Service officer (1975-1977) → New
  Mexico state epidemiologist (1977-1984) → founded and directed Project
  SIDA in Kinshasa, Zaire (from March 1984), one of the first major
  international AIDS research collaborations in Africa → founding director
  of WHO's Global Programme on AIDS (1986-1990), which he grew from a
  two-person office into one of WHO's largest programs, with an annual
  budget exceeding $100 million, before resigning in 1990 in protest of
  what he saw as an inadequate UN-level response to the pandemic → founded
  Doctors of the World-USA (now HealthRight International) in 1990 →
  returned to Harvard's School of Public Health, becoming in 1993 the
  inaugural François-Xavier Bagnoud Professor of Health and Human Rights
  and founding director of the Bagnoud (now FXB) Center for Health and
  Human Rights → became founding dean of the new School of Public Health at
  Allegheny University of the Health Sciences in 1998, shortly before his
  death.
  — Harvard FXB Center (same URL as above); Wikipedia, cross-checked
  against the Harvard and CSTE sources below: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Mann_(physician)
