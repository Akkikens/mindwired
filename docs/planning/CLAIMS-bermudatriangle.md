## ACT 1 — THE MYTH MACHINE (how "Bermuda Triangle" became a name)

- **[CONFIRMED]** No two foundational sources agree on the Triangle's shape.
  George X. Sand (1952): Florida–Bermuda–Puerto Rico. Vincent Gaddis (1964):
  same three points, closing leg through the Bahamas. Later authors stretched it
  to the Gulf of Mexico or as far as the Azores. There is no official, surveyed,
  or legally defined boundary anywhere. Sources: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_Triangle;
  legacy.geog.ucsb.edu/the-geography-of-the-bermuda-triangle.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Earliest precursor: **Edward Van Winkle Jones**, AP wire story,
  *Miami Herald*, "Sea's Puzzles Still Baffle Men In Pushbutton Age," Sept 16-17
  1950 — tallies ~135 lost, frames it as ocean-is-dangerous human interest, does
  **not** use the phrase "Bermuda Triangle" and proposes nothing paranormal.
  Primary: physics.smu.edu/pseudo/BermudaTriangle/evwjones.html.
- **[CONFIRMED]** **George X. Sand**, "Sea Mystery at Our Back Door," *Fate*
  magazine, October 1952 — first to describe the zone AS a triangle (unnamed).
  Covers Flight 19, the freighter *Sandra* (1950), BSAA's *Star Tiger* (1948) and
  *Star Ariel* (1949), and a Dec 1948 DC-3.
- **[CONFIRMED]** **Vincent Gaddis coins "Bermuda Triangle"** in "The Deadly
  Bermuda Triangle," *Argosy*, February 1964, cover story pp.28-29, 116-118.
  Draws the triangle Florida–Bermuda–Puerto Rico–(Bahamas)–Florida, implies the
  losses are "beyond the laws of chance" — **without ever comparing to actual
  shipping/flight traffic volume** (no denominator = no way to know if the count
  is unusual — a debunking point to state explicitly). Primary:
  physics.smu.edu/pseudo/BermudaTriangle/vincentgaddis.txt.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Gaddis expands into **"Invisible Horizons: True Mysteries of the
  Sea"** (1965) — the Argosy piece becomes Ch.13, "The Triangle of Death." This
  book, not the magazine article, is what later authors cite as an established
  source.
- **[CONFIRMED]** **Allan W. Eckert, "The Mystery of the Lost Patrol,"** American
  Legion Magazine, April 1962 — INVENTS dramatized Flight 19 radio dialogue
  ("looking strange," compasses "going haywire") never in the real Navy
  transcripts. Later Triangle authors lifted this fabricated dialogue as if real.
  Concrete, documentable proof of the "authors copying each other's errors"
  mechanism. Source: skepticalinquirer.org/2015/11/the-bermuda-triangle-mystery-delusion.
- **[CONFIRMED title/author/year; sales figures ALLEGED]** **Charles Berlitz,
  "The Bermuda Triangle"** (Doubleday, 1974) — the mass-market bestseller that
  cements the legend in pop culture, adds the Atlantis-connection speculation
  (zero physical evidence — attribute to Berlitz only, never assert). Reportedly
  sold in the range of millions of copies (exact "20 million" figure unverified
  against primary publisher data — correction #8).
- **[CONFIRMED]** **Larry Kusche** — a reference librarian at Arizona State
  University's Hayden Library (not a professional debunker), a licensed pilot,
  began investigating after fielding student research requests. With colleague
  Debbie Blouin, wrote directly to the US Navy, Coast Guard, Lloyd's of London,
  the National Weather Service, and read contemporary newspaper microfilm, plus
  ~92 personal interviews. Result: **"The Bermuda Triangle Mystery: Solved"**
  (Harper & Row US / New English Library UK, 1975, 302pp).
- **[CONFIRMED — Kusche's findings]** Specific, checkable problems he found in
  the popular retellings: storms/hurricanes active at the time of a loss were
  routinely omitted or misreported as calm; at least one "mystery" loss was
  actually in the Pacific, not the Atlantic, and simply carried forward
  uncorrected; a plane reported as having "disappeared" in 1937 had actually
  crashed off Daytona Beach in front of witnesses; some "lost" vessels had
  turned up safely in port, a fact dropped from retellings; no primary record
  could be found that the oft-repeated "ghost ship" *Ellen Austin* story ever
  happened at all.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Kusche's overall verdict: the "mystery" is manufactured by each
  author relying on the previous author's account instead of primary records,
  compounding errors, omissions, and outright inventions across decades of
  retelling — never independently rechecked against Navy/Coast Guard/insurance/
  weather records until Kusche did it.
- **[CONFIRMED — the single best debunking beat]** The **Mary Celeste** (1872) is
  routinely included in Triangle books despite having no geographic connection —
  the brigantine was found abandoned Dec 4 1872 roughly 400 nautical miles from
  the **Azores**, thousands of miles from the Triangle's usual boundary. Likely
  source of the confusion: a different, similarly-named ship, the paddle steamer
  *Mari Celeste*, genuinely wrecked near Bermuda in 1864. Leading mundane
  explanation for the real Mary Celeste: an alcohol-vapor buildup/leak from her
  cargo of 1,700+ barrels of denatured alcohol likely panicked the crew into a
  lifeboat evacuation that was then lost — DISPUTED but far more plausible than
  anything paranormal; no fraud or foul play was ever proven despite period
  insurance-fraud suspicion of the owners.

**Chronology for the script:** 1950 (Jones, AP) → 1952 (Sand, Fate) → 1962
(Eckert's fabricated dialogue) → 1964 (Gaddis names it) → 1965 (Gaddis's book) →
1974 (Berlitz's bestseller) → 1975 (Kusche's debunking).

## ACT 2 — THE CASES (what actually happened, and what investigators actually found)

### Flight 19 — December 5, 1945 [the cold-open case]

- **[CONFIRMED, primary source]** Five TBM/TBM-3 Avenger torpedo bombers, **14
  crew** (not 15 — FT-81 flew with only one crewman, William Earl Lightfoot,
  instead of two; the man reportedly excused, Cpl. Allan Kosnar USMC, is
  secondary-sourced only), departed **NAS Fort Lauderdale ~1410-1414R (2:10pm),
  December 5, 1945** on **"Navigation Problem No. 1"** — a specific bombing run +
  overwater navigation exercise, exact route documented in the primary findings.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Flight leader: **Lt. Charles Carroll Taylor, USNR** — 2,509.3
  total flight hours, a combat tour as a carrier torpedo pilot off USS Hancock,
  and — the irony worth using in the cold open — had been based at **Key West,
  the real Florida Keys**, before transferring to Fort Lauderdale weeks earlier.
- **[CONFIRMED]** By mid-afternoon Taylor was uncertain of his position. At
  ~1600R another instructor (name given as "Fox" in the primary transcript,
  "Cox" in most secondary Navy-history sources — correction #3) made radio
  contact. By 1611R, Fort Lauderdale learned FT-28 was "uncertain to his
  position." A 1750R high-frequency direction-finding fix placed the flight
  within 100 miles of 29°15′N, 79°00′W — well out over the Atlantic, north of
  the Bahamas, nowhere near the Keys Taylor believed he was over. Flight
  "unaccounted for since about 1900R" (7:00pm).
- **[CONFIRMED — verbatim, well-corroborated secondary/Navy-history sourcing,
  not the literal primary-document text]** Radio lines repeated consistently by
  the Navy's own history office: an unnamed crewman — *"I don't know where we
  are. We must have got lost after that last turn."* Cox/Fox to Taylor — *"FT-28,
  this is FT-74, what is your trouble?"* Taylor — *"Both of my compasses are out,
  and I am trying to find Fort Lauderdale, Florida... I am sure I'm in the Keys
  but I don't know how far down and I don't know how to get to Fort Lauderdale."*
  Taylor's final transmission — *"All planes close up tight... we'll have to
  ditch unless landfall... when the first plane drops below ten gallons, we all
  go down together."*
- **[FABRICATED — correction #1]** The "they look like they're not even Navy
  planes"/outer-space line never happened. Good to name and debunk on screen.
- **[CONFIRMED]** The Navy's search plane, **PBM-5 Mariner "Training 49"** (BuNo
  59225, 13 crew), departed NAS Banana River ~1927R to search for Flight 19;
  routine "out" report ~1930R; no further contact. At **2115R** the tanker **SS
  Gaines Mills** reported (verbatim, primary finding #54): *"observed burst of
  flames, apparently explosion, leaping flames 100 feet high burning ten minutes.
  Position 28°59′N, 80°25′W... passing through big pool of oil... Stopped,
  circled area using search lights, looking for survivors. None found."* USS
  Solomons independently reported losing radar contact with an aircraft at the
  same time/place. Accepted mundane explanation: a mid-air fuel-vapor explosion
  — PBM Mariners had a documented history of fuel-line leaks in the enclosed bomb
  bay. Total lost that night: **27** (14 + 13).
- **[CONFIRMED]** Original Navy Board finding: Taylor "became so hopelessly
  confused as to have suffered something akin to mental aberration" — effectively
  pilot-error. His mother, Katherine Taylor, petitioned the Board for Correction
  of Naval Records; in **1947** the Navy amended the record to **"cause unknown."**
  This is NOT an admission of anything paranormal — it reflects no wreckage was
  ever found to conclusively prove the navigational-error hypothesis, even though
  it remains the overwhelming circumstantial explanation.
- Sensitivity: no living survivors/accused; Taylor's mother's campaign is
  fair, decades-old public record. Treat Taylor's competence carefully — a real
  named person whose own Navy record was formally amended in his favor.

### USS Cyclops — March 1918

- **[CONFIRMED]** Navy collier, departed Rio de Janeiro Feb 16 1918, unscheduled
  Barbados stop (cracked cylinder), departed Barbados **March 4, 1918** for
  Baltimore carrying 10,800 long tons of manganese ore — over rated capacity.
  Never seen again. **306 aboard**, including the US consul general to Brazil.
  No wreckage ever found.
- **[CONFIRMED]** NHHC's current official position: cause **unknown/
  undetermined** — "many theories... none that satisfactorily accounts for her
  disappearance" (correction #9: storm is the leading GUESS, not a conclusion).
- **[DISPUTED, leading theory]** Overloading with corrosive manganese ore may
  have weakened structural I-beams; a storm (30-40kt winds recorded near the
  Virginia Capes March 10 1918) could have broken an already-weakened hull.
- **[ALLEGED, functionally debunked]** WWI German raider/submarine — German
  authorities denied knowledge during and after the war; postwar record review
  found no U-boats in the area at the time. Present as a period rumor that
  didn't hold up, not a live possibility.
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Mutiny/sabotage tied to the acting captain, a German-born
  officer — no official finding supports this; flag as speculative folklore only.
- Sensitivity: 1918, no identifiable living relatives found; 306 dead is a
  large loss — avoid speculative blame on the named officer beyond documented fact.

### SS Marine Sulphur Queen — February 1963

- **[CONFIRMED]** T2 tanker (built 1944 as SS Esso New Haven, converted to carry
  molten sulphur). Departed Beaumont, TX Feb 2 1963 for Norfolk, VA. Last contact
  Feb 4 1963 in the Florida Straits. **All 39 crew lost**, no bodies recovered;
  some debris found off the Florida Keys.
- **[CONFIRMED — official finding]** US Coast Guard Marine Board of Investigation
  (convened Feb 20 1963): the ship was **"unsafe and should not have sailed."**
  Documented deficiencies: routine tank-area fires so common officers stopped
  sounding alarms for them; inoperable temperature gauges; a ruptured steam coil;
  worn shaft packing; the "weak back" structural flaw common to converted T2
  tankers; a scheduled drydock inspection postponed by owners over scheduling.
  Board named four plausible mechanisms (cargo explosion, hull failure,
  synchronous-roll capsize, steam explosion) but stated: *"in the absence of
  survivors or physical remains... the exact cause... cannot be determined."*
- **[CONFIRMED — a second great debunking beat]** The ship sank in the **Gulf of
  Mexico/Florida Straits — not inside the traditionally-drawn Triangle at all.**
  Gaddis's 1964 Argosy article was first to brand it a Triangle "victim,"
  reducing a documented maintenance/structural failure to "sailed into the
  unknown."
- Sensitivity: HIGH relative to the other cases — 1963 is within living memory;
  children/widows of the 39 could plausibly be alive. The Coast Guard's finding
  places responsibility on the ship's owners/maintenance, not the crew — frame
  the crew as victims of a known-unsafe vessel.

### BSAA Star Tiger (1948) / Star Ariel (1949) — supporting material

- **[CONFIRMED]** Star Tiger, Jan 30 1948, Avro Tudor IV, Azores→Bermuda, 31 lost
  incl. WWII RAF hero Air Marshal Sir Arthur Coningham. UK Court of Inquiry:
  *"the fate of Star Tiger must remain an unsolved mystery."*
- **[CONFIRMED]** Star Ariel, Jan 17 1949, same aircraft type, Bermuda→Kingston,
  20 lost. Official report: *"through lack of evidence due to no wreckage having
  been found, the cause of the accident is unknown."* No mechanical defect,
  weather issue, or sabotage found in a 55,000-sq-mile search.
- Both are real, genuinely unsolved aviation losses (honest "cause unknown," not
  evidence of anything anomalous) — useful contrast cases if the episode wants a
  British-aviation beat alongside Flight 19.

## ACT 3 — WHY THE MYSTERY ISN'T ONE (statistics / real science)

- **[CONFIRMED]** **NOAA National Ocean Service** (live, direct quote):
  *"There is no evidence that mysterious disappearances occur with any greater
  frequency in the Bermuda Triangle than in any other large, well-traveled area
  of the ocean."* Source: oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/bermudatri.html. NOAA
  attributes real incidents to hurricanes/tropical storms, the Gulf Stream's
  capacity for rapid violent weather changes, and shallow reef/shoal water —
  and explicitly calls paranormal explanations "whimsical ideas" with no evidence.
- **[CONFIRMED]** **US Coast Guard / Navy** — documented (via Kusche's 1975
  correspondence, repeated by NHHC): standard reply is that ordinary physical
  causes explain the incidents; widely-quoted line: *"It has been our experience
  that the combined forces of nature and the unpredictability of mankind outdo
  science-fiction stories many times each year."* The **US Board on Geographic
  Names** does not recognize "Bermuda Triangle" as an official place name at all.
- **[CONFIRMED — a load-bearing fact]** **Lloyd's of London does not charge
  elevated premiums** for vessels transiting the area, and their own loss
  records never supported the legend. Frame this precisely: the entity whose
  entire business model depends on correctly pricing risk sees no elevated risk
  here — stronger evidence than any government denial.
- **[CONFIRMED — the strongest single statistic]** A **2013 World Wildlife Fund
  study**, using real Lloyd's List Intelligence shipping-loss data (1999-2011),
  ranked ocean regions by total losses and loss density. **The Bermuda Triangle
  did not make the top 10 most dangerous shipping areas** — it placed roughly
  12th of 35 by total losses and 22nd of 35 by loss density, well behind the
  South China Sea, the eastern Mediterranean/Black Sea, and the North Sea.
  Source: Christian Science Monitor, June 10 2013, reporting the WWF study.
  **This is the episode's central "statistically nothing" data point** — a real,
  independent, ranked dataset, not just a denial.
- **[CONFIRMED — the count-vs-rate argument]** The region overlaps some of the
  densest civilian traffic corridors in the Western Hemisphere: PortMiami and
  Port Canaveral together handled **~17 million cruise passengers in FY2025**;
  San Juan's airport boards roughly 12,000 passengers a day. More traffic
  mechanically produces more absolute incidents even at a flat or below-average
  rate — exactly what Kusche, NOAA, the Coast Guard, and the WWF study
  independently found. Sources: Maritime Executive/CBS Miami/Royal Caribbean
  Blog/Time Out (Dec 2025 port-authority reporting); The Points Guy (SJU).
- **[CONFIRMED]** The **Gulf Stream** — a genuine ~2 m/s current — both breeds
  sudden severe weather (NOAA's own explanation) AND disperses wreckage/debris
  quickly, a mundane physical reason searches often find nothing — not evidence
  of anything paranormal.
- **[CONFIRMED]** Human error, given traffic volume, is statistically sufficient
  on its own. Dr. Karl Kruszelnicki (2017): *"The number [of ships and planes]
  that go missing in the Bermuda Triangle is the same as anywhere in the world
  on a percentage basis."*
- **[CONFIRMED]** Underwater topology: most of the seafloor here sits ~19,000ft
  down; near the southern tip, the real **Puerto Rico Trench** plunges to the
  **Milwaukee Deep (~27,493ft / 8,380m)** — the deepest point in the Atlantic.
  Extreme depth is a genuine, boring reason wreck recovery is sometimes
  impossible — not a supernatural one.

### The theories — attributed to their proponents, never asserted as real

- **[ALLEGED theory / CONFIRMED debunk]** Methane hydrate eruptions
  (destabilizing buoyancy or igniting gas clouds). USGS geologist **Bill
  Dillon**: no evidence of any large-scale gas hydrate release in this region
  for roughly **15,000 years**. Real geological process, no evidence it has
  happened here in the modern era.
- **[CONFIRMED phenomenon, DISPUTED significance]** Magnetic compass variation
  (declination) — real, basic navigational science every sailor/pilot corrects
  for; the "only place on Earth" framing is a popular exaggeration (agonic
  lines exist at multiple longitudes and drift over time).
- **[ALLEGED/DISPUTED]** Rogue waves — real oceanographer (Dr. Simon Boxall,
  University of Southampton) modeled storm-convergence rogue waves up to ~30m
  in a TV documentary; legitimate wave physics, but nothing about it is unique
  to this specific patch of ocean versus any other storm-convergence zone.
- **[DISPUTED — the best "how the myth machine still runs" beat]** Hexagonal
  "air bomb" clouds. Meteorologist **Dr. Randy Cerveny** gave a Science Channel
  program an illustrative "straw man" explanation about microburst-generating
  cloud formations — the show's producers presented it as a solved mystery.
  Cerveny told the Washington Post: *"the editing on this was horrendous."*
  These hexagonal formations occur at many ocean locations worldwide, not just
  here. **Use this on screen explicitly as a case study of how the myth gets
  manufactured for TV, using the scientist's own on-record complaint.**
- **[EXCLUDE — do not use in any form]** A circulating claim of a "University
  of Miami/Woods Hole" sonar survey finding an "unnaturally glassy seafloor"
  in a precise 12-nautical-mile equilateral triangle traces to no identifiable
  real study, institution, or named researcher — pattern-matches a fabricated/
  AI-generated hoax article. Worth naming in the episode as a LIVE example that
  the myth-machine Gaddis started in 1964 is still running today, but never as
  a real finding.

## COLD OPEN — locked, primary-source anchored

Per the Fern cold-open formula (dated moment, named location, small concrete
action) and confirmed facts only (no disputed dialogue in the opening beat):

> *December fifth, nineteen forty-five. Fort Lauderdale, Florida. Lieutenant
> Charles Taylor climbs into a torpedo bomber for a routine training flight —
> the same kind of flight he's led dozens of times before.*

Anchor facts used (all primary-confirmed): exact date, real location, Taylor's
name/rank, the routine nature of the mission. The irony beat (he'd flown out of
the REAL Florida Keys during the war) is a strong, verified detail to land soon
after in the same scene — this is what makes his later belief that he was "over
the Keys" so devastating rather than baffling.
