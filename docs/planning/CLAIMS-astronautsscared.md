## Final ranked list — 12 entries (footage status per entry)

Legend: 🧍 living, ⚰ deceased. Footage: **video** (real motion footage
exists) / **photo** (real stills only) / **dossier** (zero real coverage,
illustrated reconstruction required).

### 1. Luca Parmitano — helmet flooding, ISS EVA-23 (July 16, 2013) 🧍
**CONFIRMED.** ESA (Italy) astronaut, EVA-23 alongside NASA's Chris Cassidy.
~1 hour into the spacewalk his EMU helmet began filling with water
(~1-1.5 liters) from a malfunctioning cooling/drink-bag system, covering
eyes/ears/nose/mouth; navigated blind and deaf back to the airlock by
memory/touch. Spacewalk terminated at 1h32m. A NASA Mishap Investigation
Board (real, on-record) found a prior smaller leak on an earlier EVA had
not been escalated — cite as NASA's own finding, not a conspiracy claim.
**Quotes (ESA "Volare" blog, his own words):** "The water has also almost
completely covered the front of my visor, sticking to it and obscuring my
vision." / "At that moment... my ability to see... completely vanishes...
I can't even be sure that the next time I breathe I will fill my lungs
with air and not liquid." / "The water is now inside my ears and I'm
completely cut off." Later (KGO-TV interview): "I experienced what it's
like to be a goldfish in a fishbowl from the point of view of the
goldfish."
**Footage: video** — real NASA helmet-cam footage exists (underlies NASA's
own "EVA 23" documentary and 10th-anniversary retrospective); exact
Commons/PD file ID to be pinned during footage fetch.

### 2. Gene Cernan — "Spacewalk From Hell," Gemini 9-A (June 5, 1966) ⚰ (d. 2017)
**CONFIRMED.** Third-ever American EVA; no handholds/restraint system, every
movement caused tumbling, suit cooling overwhelmed (heart rate ~180bpm),
visor fogged then partially froze in Earth's shadow, lost ~13lbs body
water. Commander Tom Stafford ordered him back inside before objectives
were complete.
**Quotes (mission audio + his memoir *The Last Man on the Moon*, chapter
titled "The Spacewalk From Hell," his own coinage):** "The snake is all
over me..." / "We're really cooking back here" / "Really fogged up here"
/ "I don't think I'll make it that way."
**Footage: video** — real archival 16mm EVA footage (NASA/archive.org),
plus Commons stills ("Gemini-9_eva-cernan.jpg").

### 3. Fred Haise — Apollo 13, freezing Lunar Module (April 13-17, 1970) 🧍 (age 92)
**CONFIRMED.** Oxygen tank explosion ~56hrs into flight; crew moved into
the LM "lifeboat," rationed power/water, endured near-freezing cabin
temps. Haise is the last surviving crew member (Lovell d. Aug 2025 — frame
in memoriam; Swigert d. 1982).
**Real exchange (correcting the movie misquote, see DATA CORRECTIONS #1):**
Swigert: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell (repeating):
"Ah, Houston, we've had a problem." **Lead with Haise instead** — his own
quote (memoir *Never Panic Early*, 2022): "We did not have adequate
clothing" — describing wearing three pairs of underwear to survive the
cold.
**Footage: video** — the strongest coverage in the entire set: real launch
footage, the iconic damaged-Service-Module photo, real Mission Control
audio/video ("Apollo in Real Time" restored feed).

### 4. Scott Parazynski — live-wire solar array repair, STS-120 (Nov 3, 2007) 🧍
**CONFIRMED.** 7hr19min EVA-4; rode the 90-ft robotic boom farther from an
airlock than any spacewalker before, cut a snagged wire and installed
stabilizers on a FULLY ENERGIZED solar array in a 100%-oxygen suit. Repair
succeeded, array deployed fully.
**Quote (BuzzFeed News, his own words):** "There was a real danger that we
could do even worse damage to the Space Station. Then there was the
potential of risk to myself, because if there was any metal to metal
connection with the solar panel, or arcing, it could actually electrocute
me or cause ignition of the 100% oxygen in my spacesuit."
**Footage: video** — real NASA/ESA TV EVA coverage; Commons
"STS-120_EVA_Scott_Parazynski.jpg," "STS-120_SAW_tear.jpg."

### 5. Neil Armstrong ⚰ (d. 2012) & David Scott 🧍 — Gemini 8 tumble (March 16, 1966)
**CONFIRMED.** After the first-ever docking in space, the joined
spacecraft began an uncommanded roll; Armstrong undocked believing the
Agena was at fault, but a stuck Gemini thruster caused the spin to
accelerate toward ~1 revolution/second — fast enough to risk blackout.
Armstrong shut down the malfunctioning system and used reentry-reserved
thrusters to stabilize, forcing an emergency Pacific splashdown under
mission rules.
**Quotes (real-time radio transcript + Scott's memoir *Two Sides of the
Moon*):** Scott: "We have serious problems here… We're tumbling end over
end. We're disengaged from the Agena." Scott's later account: "With our
vision beginning to blur… locating the right switch was not simple."
**Footage: video** — real docking-approach + Pacific splashdown/recovery
footage (Commons "Recovery of the Gemini 8 mission" subcategory); the
tumble itself has no camera coverage — pair the transcript with the real
surrounding footage, do not imply video of the spin itself exists.

### 6. Boris Volynov — Soyuz 5 nose-first reentry (Jan 18, 1969) 🧍 — **dossier: true**
**DISPUTED technical cause, CONFIRMED outcome** (see DATA CORRECTIONS #8).
After undocking from Soyuz 4, the service module failed to separate;
descent module reentered hatch-first at ~5,000°F, filling the cabin with
smoke, before the service module burned/broke away just before
catastrophic failure. Chutes partially tangled, hard landing broke
several teeth, landed ~2,000km off-target in the Urals at −40°C; local
peasants sheltered him. Story suppressed by Soviet authorities for
decades.
**Quotes (per Ben Evans/AmericaSpace's sourcing — attribute as historian
account, not primary document):** Repeated to himself during the tumble:
"No panic, no panic." First words to rescuers, through broken teeth: "Is
my hair gray?"
**Footage: dossier** — zero real photo/video of the reentry, smoke, or
landing exists (Soviet secrecy, no camera present) — the one illustrated
DossierScene reconstruction beat in this episode. Pair with real,
available Soyuz-4/5-era portrait/diagram photos around it, never for the
reconstructed moment itself.

### 7. Chris Hadfield — Mir docking sensor failure, STS-74 (Nov 12, 1995) 🧍
**CONFIRMED.** Second shuttle-Mir docking; range/rate sensors failed ~30
seconds before contact, forcing the crew to judge final approach by eye
and stopwatch — a hard or crooked docking risked killing Mir's 3-person
crew.
**Quote (BuzzFeed News, his own words):** "Hit too hard or crooked and we
could have broken the space station, killing its crew. It was an
incredibly tense final 10 minutes." / "If you hit Mir just a little too
hard, then you would break Mir in half and kill the three people on
board."
**Footage: video** — rare real IMAX 65mm footage of the actual docking
approach exists (NTRS "STS-74 Mission Highlights Resource Tape"). Used
instead of Hadfield's more famous STS-100 blindness story to avoid the
over-told beat (see DATA CORRECTIONS #11) — his STS-100 story can still
appear as a brief mid-list nod, not the lead.

### 8. Vasily Tsibliyev & Alexander Lazutkin — Mir-Progress collision (June 25, 1997) 🧍🧍
**CONFIRMED.** Manual TORU-system test docking of an unpiloted Progress
craft struck Mir's Spektr module, puncturing the hull; crew had minutes to
cut cable bundles and seal the hatch before dangerous depressurization.
American astronaut Michael Foale also aboard, assisted.
**Quotes (TIME 1997 "A Bad Day in Space," NASA SP-4225):** Tsibliyev,
before the collision: "This is a bad business, Sasha" / "It's a dangerous
thing to do." Lazutkin, on impact: "I saw it hit. It was Spektr." On the
approaching craft: "[it looked] full of menace, like a shark... at that
point there was a great thump and the whole station shook."
**Footage: photo** — real NASA/ESA photos of the damage exist (taken by
STS-86's crew 3 months later); no video confirmed of the collision moment
itself.

### 9. Mike Mullane 🧍(apparently) & Hoot Gibson 🧍 — tile damage, STS-27 (Dec 2-6, 1988)
**CONFIRMED.** Classified DoD mission; ascent debris strike damaged 700+
heat-shield tiles, one torn away to bare metal; ground engineers
underestimated damage from a degraded encrypted downlink. During reentry
plasma melted through a steel mounting plate directly under the missing
tile before the shuttle passed peak heating — the same failure mode that
later destroyed Columbia (2003), except this time it held.
**Quotes (Mullane's memoir *Riding Rockets*; Gibson via AmericaSpace):**
Mullane: "at least one tile had been completely blasted from the
fuselage" / anticipating reentry: "we were dead men floating." Gibson,
privately believing Atlantis might not survive: "No use dying all
tensed-up."
**Footage: video** — Commons "STS-27metalmelt.jpg" (the actual melted
plate) + real ground OPF tile-damage inspection footage.

### 10. Pete Conrad, Alan Bean, Dick Gordon — struck by lightning twice, Apollo 12 (Nov 14, 1969) ⚰⚰⚰ (all deceased)
**CONFIRMED.** Saturn V struck by lightning at 36.5s and ~52s after
liftoff, knocking out fuel cells/telemetry ("all eleven system warning
lights fired at the same time"). Mission Control's John Aaron recalled an
obscure switch setting ("SCE to AUX"); Alan Bean uniquely remembered its
location and flipped it, restoring telemetry.
**Quotes (mission transcript, Smithsonian):** Conrad: "The hell was that."
Gordon: "What the hell was that?" Conrad, on the instrument panel: "the
whole board looks like a Christmas tree." Post-mission, Conrad joked: "I
think we need to do a little more all-weather testing!"
**Footage: video, exceptionally strong** — real archive.org launch footage
shows the actual lightning strikes on camera.

### 11. Peggy Whitson, Yuri Malenchenko, Yi So-yeon — ballistic reentry, Soyuz TMA-11 (April 19, 2008) 🧍🧍🧍
**CONFIRMED.** Service module again failed to separate cleanly after
deorbit burn, forcing a steep unplanned ballistic reentry at up to 8.2-10g
(vs normal ~4-5g), landing 260-420km off-target on the Kazakh steppe. Yi
struck by a loose bag, required physical therapy; Whitson/Malenchenko
uninjured.
**Quotes (Whitson, post-landing; Yi So-yeon, CBS News):** Whitson: "I saw
8.2 Gs on the meter, and it was pretty dramatic" / "Gravity's not really
my friend right now." Yi So-yeon: "During descent I saw some kind of fire
outside... At first I was really scared because it looked really, really
hot and I thought we could burn." / "I looked at the others and I
pretended to be OK."
**Footage: photo (unconfirmed exact files)** — recovery-scene photos very
likely exist given the major news coverage, but exact file IDs were not
pinned in research — **verify directly during footage fetch before
scripting on-screen citation**; no interior reentry video exists.

### 12. Andrew Feustel — anti-fog eye scare, STS-134 EVA-3 (May 25, 2011) 🧍
**CONFIRMED.** Anti-fog compound flaked into his eye mid-spacewalk,
causing acute stinging/watering — the same hazard category as Hadfield's
2001 incident, a decade later (NASA technical report notes this occurred
on "at least seven" EMU EVAs — a real, unresolved recurring design issue).
**Quote (live air-to-ground radio call, widely reported):** "Just an FYI,
my right eye is stinging like crazy right now. It's watering a lot."
Resolved by rubbing his eye against the helmet's interior foam block;
completed the spacewalk.
**Footage: video** — real-time CBS EVA-3 coverage of the actual call; NASA
mission gallery IDs (S134-E-008955, ISS028-E-005266) to be cross-checked
against Commons during fetch.
