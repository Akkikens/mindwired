## Description (paste-ready as one block — English only)

```
Somewhere on Mount Everest's northeast ridge, in a small limestone alcove at 8,500 meters, there's a body climbers have walked past for thirty years. They called him Green Boots, for the bright green mountaineering boots still on his feet. It's the only thing about him nobody argued over — because for three decades, everyone had his real identity wrong.

This is the real, sourced story of the bodies still on Mount Everest: the 1996 storm that killed eight people in a single night on both sides of the mountain, the wife who has waited thirty years for her husband's body to come home, the century-old mystery of George Mallory and Andrew Irvine, and the 2026 identification that finally corrected one name — while leaving another man's real body still unaccounted for.

More than 200 bodies are estimated to remain on Everest today — an estimate, not an actual count, traced back to a single 2015 magazine article and repeated everywhere since. What's actually documented: over 300 deaths on the mountain since 1922, most of them Nepali guides and workers whose names rarely make headlines. Above 8,000 meters, the "death zone," a body doesn't decompose — it mummifies, preserved by the same cold that killed it. Recovering even one costs $40,000 to $80,000 and up to ten climbers days of work.

We tell both halves of the May 10, 1996 disaster — Rob Hall and Scott Fischer's teams on the south side (the story "Into Thin Air" made famous), and the Indo-Tibetan Border Police team that died the same night on the north side, whose story almost nobody tells. One of those three Indian climbers became "Green Boots" — misidentified as Tsewang Paljor for thirty years, until India's Border Police said this year that 2024 DNA evidence identifies him as Lance Naik Dorje Morup instead. The full lab report has never been made public; other reporting says the case currently rests on gear, radio logs, and eyewitness testimony, with full DNA testing planned only once his remains are finally brought home. Either way, one fact hasn't changed: Tsewang Paljor's real body is still up there, unaccounted for.

We also cover George Mallory and Andrew Irvine's 1924 disappearance, Mallory's body found in 1999, and the 2024 discovery that may finally be Irvine's remains, 100 years later — the camera that could prove whether either man summited before Hillary has still never been found. And we cover Hannelore Schmatz (1979) and David Sharp (2006), whose deaths reshaped the entire debate about what climbers owe each other on the mountain.

Every fact in this documentary is sourced and hedged where the record itself is contested — see Sources below.
```
