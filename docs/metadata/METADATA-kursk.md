## Description (paste as ONE unit)

---

On August 12, 2000, seismometers in three countries recorded two explosions in the Barents Sea, two minutes and ten seconds apart. Russia's most famous submarine — the "unsinkable" K-141 Kursk — was on the bottom with 118 men. Moscow said the crew died instantly. Then divers recovered Captain-Lieutenant Dmitri Kolesnikov's body, and in his pocket, two water-soaked pages: "There are 23 of us here." This is the full evidence breakdown: what Moscow said, hour by hour, against what the record shows was actually true.

Inside: the real NORSAR seismic record of both explosions (shown as an exhibit, from the official Norwegian government report on the accident), the documented false statements — "radio contact," "air being supplied," the "60-degree lean" that measured 1.5 degrees — the five days of refused British and Norwegian help, the Priz capsule failures, the Vidyayevo meetings, the Kolesnikov note and the second note, the 2001 salvage, the Ustinov report's own words ("stunning breaches of discipline") and its zero criminal charges, and the aftermath nobody covers: how the Kursk coverage triggered the state takeover of ORT — what the families' lawyer calls the place where the lies began.

Every claim in this video traces to the public record: the Norwegian Radiation Protection Authority report (StrålevernRapport 2001:5, reproducing NORSAR data), the published summary of the 2002 Ustinov investigation, contemporaneous reporting, and the Kremlin's own published transcripts and photographs. The Kolesnikov note is rendered as a clearly labeled RECONSTRUCTION — the original has never been fully made public. The NATO-collision theory is reported as the claim of named Russian officials; the official investigation concluded the cause was a faulty weld in a high-test-peroxide practice torpedo. Survival-duration claims beyond the official six-to-eight-hour finding are attributed to the families and their lawyer.
