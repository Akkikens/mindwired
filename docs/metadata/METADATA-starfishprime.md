July 9, 1962. The United States detonated a 1.4-megaton nuclear warhead 250 miles above the Pacific Ocean — on purpose. What happened in the next few seconds knocked out streetlights 900 miles away in Honolulu, fried a British satellite, and created a radiation belt that lingered in orbit for years. This is the real story of Starfish Prime, the day America nuked space.

A Thor missile carried the W49 warhead to 400 kilometers altitude before it detonated. Within two seconds, an electromagnetic pulse reached Hawaii — about 300 streetlights went dark, burglar alarms triggered, and a telephone relay link between islands was damaged. From Honolulu, witnesses saw the sky ignite into an artificial aurora that lasted for minutes. It was, in the words of one 1962 newspaper, as if "the heavens had belched forth a new sun."

The damage didn't stop at Hawaii. The blast created an artificial radiation belt that killed off at least a third of the satellites then in low Earth orbit within months — including Ariel 1, Britain's first satellite, and eventually Telstar 1, which took seven months to die from accumulated radiation damage. A year later, the Limited Test Ban Treaty banned nuclear tests in space — Starfish Prime was one of the reasons why.

And it isn't just history. Since 2024, US officials have warned that Russia is developing a space-based nuclear weapon designed to do exactly what Starfish Prime did in 1962 — except now, instead of two dozen satellites in orbit, there are thousands, including the entire Starlink constellation. The Cold War's most reckless experiment is suddenly relevant again.
