## DESCRIPTION (paste as one unit)

Ten machines from the 1970s and 80s remain the only proof anyone has ever collected from the surface of Venus — and under every space agency's current plan, they may stay the only ones for another decade. This is the full story of the Soviet Venera program: the machines built, quite literally, to die a little further each time, the parachutes deliberately shrunk so a probe would reach crush depth before its battery ran out, and the titanium capsule that finally survived long enough to send back the first photographs ever taken from another planet's surface.

It's also the story of a real, credentialed Venera-program veteran who looked at those photographs in 2012 and became convinced something in them was alive — and the careful, patient debunking that followed, image by image. And it closes on a fight happening in the U.S. Congress right now, in 2026, over whether America will ever fund a mission that photographs Venus's surface again.

Every image in this video is real: Wikimedia Commons archival photographs, the actual Venera panoramas from 1975 and 1982, real museum lander replicas, Soviet-era postage stamps, a 2025 Russian commemorative coin, and NASA's own Magellan radar footage. No AI-generated imagery. Sources: Andrew LePage (Drew Ex Machina), NASA/JPL, the Planetary Society, Wikipedia (Venera program pages, cross-checked against NSSDCA and Siddiqi's NASA history SP-2018-4041), and the 2012 debunk coverage from Space.com, Live Science, and The Planetary Society.
