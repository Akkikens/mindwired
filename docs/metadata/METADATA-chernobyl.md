## Description (English ONLY)
In 1986, the Soviet Union told the world Chernobyl was the operators' fault.
Six years later, a second, far less publicized report from the International
Atomic Energy Agency quietly reversed that finding — this time pointing at the
reactor's own design, and a flaw the men in that control room were never told
existed.

This documentary is built around that real report — INSAG-7, published in 1992
— shown on screen, alongside the actual physics behind the disaster: the
positive void coefficient that made the reactor speed up, not slow down, as it
lost cooling water, and the flawed control rods that turned an emergency
shutdown into a power spike. We walk the full night of April 26th, 1986, the
men who died trying to save a reactor they believed was still intact, and the
36-hour delay, the continued May Day parade, and the Swedish radiation alarms
that finally forced the truth into the open.

We also do something most retellings don't: we compare the real record to HBO's
2019 miniseries, scene by scene where it matters — what the show got right (the
physics, the horror of the rooftop cleanup), what it openly acknowledges is
fictional (a composite scientist character), and where its five-hour Vienna
account leans closer to the 1986 story than the 1992 correction. This isn't a
takedown of the show — its own writer has spoken honestly about the choices he
made. It's simply what the official record adds.

A note on fairness: engineer Anatoly Dyatlov was convicted in 1987; he spent the
rest of his life disputing his personal culpability, and the 1992 report's
design findings support at least part of his argument, without fully clearing
him. Long-term health estimates from the disaster range enormously by
methodology — from around 4,000 to over 90,000 depending on who's counting and
how — and we present that range honestly rather than picking one number.
Everything here is grounded in the real INSAG-7 report, IAEA/WHO/UNSCEAR
findings, and named, attributed sourcing throughout.
