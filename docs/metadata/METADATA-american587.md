## Description (paste as ONE unit)

---

November 12, 2001. Sixty-two days after September 11th, an American Airlines Airbus A300 bound for Santo Domingo took off from Kennedy Airport and, about two minutes later, came down on Belle Harbor, Queens — a neighborhood that had already lost about 65 residents at the World Trade Center. 265 people died, five of them on the ground. Within minutes the bridges and tunnels were closed and fighter jets were scrambled. New York thought it was happening again. It wasn't. What tore the airplane's entire tail off — found in Jamaica Bay, roughly a mile from the main wreckage — took twenty-two seconds, and three years later split the National Transportation Safety Board three votes to two over who was to blame.

Inside: the departure behind a Japan Airlines 747 that left one minute and forty seconds earlier — less than the two-minute spacing rule, more than the four miles it also allows, and fully within the rules according to the NTSB; the cockpit voice recorder's "little wake turbulence, huh?"; the rudder pedals that, at 250 knots, needed just 32 pounds of foot pressure and 1.2 inches of travel to reach full deflection; the fin that failed at about twice its certified limit load; the airline training program and the training video the NTSB examined; the same-day terrorism rumor and how fast it was ruled out; the 3–2 vote that reordered the contributing factors; and the fix the FAA ordered eleven years later, after concluding that "training alone is inadequate."

The NTSB's probable cause (AAR-04/04, adopted October 26, 2004) is the in-flight separation of the vertical stabilizer from loads beyond ultimate design created by the first officer's unnecessary and excessive rudder pedal inputs, with the Airbus A300-600 rudder system design and elements of American Airlines' Advanced Aircraft Maneuvering Program as contributing factors. This video presents the first officer's inputs as the NTSB's finding, not as our judgment of him. That finding was contested: the Allied Pilots Association asked the NTSB to reopen the investigation in 2006, and the first officer's father disputed it until his death in 2014. American Airlines argued Airbus had not shared what it knew about the rudder system; Airbus said the pilot was acting on training he received. Board Member Carol Carmody, joined by Member Richard Healing, dissented from the final ordering of the contributing factors. Every cockpit and tower line in this video is a labeled CVR/ATC RECREATION, read verbatim from the NTSB's official transcripts — the public docket contains no audio.
