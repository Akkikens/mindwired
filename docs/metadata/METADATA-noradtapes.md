## DESCRIPTION (English only)
At 8:24 and 38 seconds on the morning of September 11, 2001, a hijacker aboard American
Airlines Flight 11 keyed the wrong microphone. What he meant for the cabin went out over
Boston Center's air traffic control frequency instead. The controller working the flight,
Pete Zalewski, had already called that aircraft twelve times with no answer — and he could
not make out the words. The official record says so plainly: he heard "something
unintelligible."

So the manager of Boston Center told the centre's quality assurance specialist to pull the
tape.

The phrase that named the worst attack in American history — "We have some planes" — was
not heard by anyone. It was recovered from a recording, by a man the public report never
names, and it did not reach the chain of command until around 9:03.

This film is about the recordings. All of them.

Because the air defense sector at Rome, New York recorded every channel on its operations
floor as a matter of routine, and three years later, when the 9/11 Commission finally
subpoenaed those tapes, what was on them did not match what the public had been told.
NORAD's own account — a press release six days after the attacks, and sworn testimony in
May 2003 — said the military was notified that United 93 was hijacked at 9:16, and that
American 77 was hijacked at 9:24, and that the Langley fighters launched in response. The
Commission found all three statements incorrect, in those words. There was no hijack to
report at 9:16. The 9:24 notice was not about Flight 77 at all.

It was about a plane that had already crashed.

We follow the whole morning through the audio: the hijack protocol that assumed the pilot
would announce it and that fighters would fly a discreet escort five miles behind; the
8:37:52 call that began the air defense of America and the question that answered it — "Is
this real-world or exercise?"; two F-15s launched from Cape Cod with nowhere to go, because
nobody could tell them where the aircraft was; a report at 9:21 that American 11 was still
airborne and heading for Washington, thirty-five minutes after it had hit the North Tower,
which the Commission could never trace to a source; the Langley fighters flying east over
the Atlantic because the scramble order contained no destination; and an order authorising
them to shoot that reached the operations floor at 10:31 — twenty-eight minutes after the
last aircraft was already down in a Pennsylvania field.

And the hinge of the whole thing: a single line in a handwritten log. At 9:24 the Mission
Crew Commander's log records "American Airlines #N334AA hijacked." November three three
four alpha alpha is a tail number, and it belongs to American 11. Somebody read that entry
later, took it for Flight 77, and built three years of public account on it.

A note on what this film does not say. Nobody was ever charged. The Commission debated a
criminal referral to the Justice Department in the summer of 2004 and declined; its general
counsel referred the matter to two Inspectors General instead; both investigated, and
neither found evidence that anyone knowingly said anything false. The Defense Department's
watchdog attributed the errors to poor investigation and record-keeping. Commissioner John
Lehman put it this way: whether it was willful or just the fog of stupid bureaucracy, he
did not know. Thomas Kean said they never found out why NORAD told them what it told them.
Where this film uses a stronger characterisation, it is quoting a named person, and the
Commission's own finding sits alongside it: an accurate understanding of that morning
reflects no discredit on the people who were on the floor. They were given bad information
and they made the right calls with it.

Four passages in this film are the actual recordings, labelled on screen: the certified FAA
re-recording of Boston Center's Sector 46 radar position — including the transmission at
8:24:38 that will not resolve, because it did not resolve for him either — and the Boston
Center call into the NEADS identification section. Everything else spoken by a voice other
than the narrator is a labelled recreation. No cockpit voice recorder audio from any of the
four flights has ever been publicly released, and none is used here. No victim audio is
used. No camera recorded the NEADS operations floor that morning; the tape is what survives.
