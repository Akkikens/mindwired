## Description (English only)
A real camera glides through the most expensive machine human beings have
ever built — 250 miles above your head, moving at five miles per second.
This is not an animation. Every piece of footage in this documentary is
real: real NASA cameras inside the International Space Station, the real
1984 speech that started it, the real launch of the first module in 1998,
and the real government plan — already signed, already funded — to steer
all 430 tons of it into the Pacific Ocean in January 2031.

This is the complete story of the International Space Station, told with
the honesty the subject deserves. It survived 22 congressional attempts to
kill it — one by a single vote, 216 to 215. Its first module was built in
Moscow but is legally American property. Its "$150 billion" price tag —
the number in this video's title — turns out to be one analyst's 2010
estimate: NASA's own audit says $75 billion through 2013, and an astronaut
who commanded the station puts the true lifetime figure at a quarter of a
trillion. Nobody actually knows. We show you why.

Inside, you'll see how the machine keeps seven people alive while
constantly trying not to: air with ten times Earth's carbon dioxide, water
recycled at 98 percent ("yesterday's coffee is tomorrow's coffee"), an acre
of solar panels riding through sixteen sunrises a day — and a piece of its
discarded battery pallet that survived reentry in 2024 and punched through
the roof of a family home in Florida. You'll see the real 2-millimeter hole
someone drilled inside a docked Soyuz — an investigation Russia sealed and
never published. The module that flipped the entire station one and a half
revolutions. The night the crew sheltered in their lifeboats from a missile
test's debris field, every 93 minutes.

And the ending: the deorbit vehicle SpaceX is building under an $843
million contract, the final crew's departure at 220 kilometers, the breakup
over Point Nemo — and the uncomfortable fact that the replacements aren't
ready, while the one station that will still be flying belongs to the
country banned from this one.

Every claim is sourced — NASA inspector general audits, GAO reports, the
2024 deorbit white paper (shown on screen), astronauts' own published
words. Where officials disagree, we show both sides. Living people are
quoted only from their own on-record statements.
