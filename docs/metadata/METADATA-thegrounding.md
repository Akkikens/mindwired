## DESCRIPTION (English only)
At 9:42 on the morning of 11 September 2001, a man on his first day in the job ordered every
aircraft in American airspace to land immediately, at the nearest airport, wherever that
happened to be. About 4,500 of them were flying at the time.

No procedure for doing that existed. Anywhere.

There was a plan on the shelf. It was called SCATANA, and its full title was "Plan for the
Security Control of Air Traffic and Air Navigation Aids" — a Cold War document written for
Soviet bombers, whose defining act was shutting off the navigation beacons so an enemy could
not use them. On a morning when four and a half thousand crews were trying to find a runway,
executing it would have blinded every one of them. It contained no instruction to land
civil traffic at all, and the authority to declare it belonged to the military, not to the
FAA. Ben Sliney, the national operations manager at the FAA's command centre in Herndon,
said it plainly years later: it was "a tool that we did not have, but we adopted."

This is the story of the only part of that morning that worked.

It was never one decision. New York Center declared "ATC zero" minutes after the second
impact — the first time that had ever been done for anything like this. Boston went on tape
saying they were shutting the city down and suggesting everyone else do the same. At 9:25
the command centre asked headquarters whether they wanted a nationwide ground stop, and
while executives were still discussing it, ordered one anyway. But a ground stop only stops
departures. It does nothing about the aircraft already in the sky.

Then 9:42, and the order that had no precedent.

We follow how it was actually executed: 700 aircraft down within minutes — the FAA says four
minutes, Sliney says ten, and this film says both — 2,800 in the first hour, the scope at
Harrisburg going from full to "almost completely black" in half an hour, and the sky finally
clear at 12:16 p.m. The written advisory suspending the national airspace system did not go
out until 11:06. The verbal order beat the paperwork by eighty-four minutes.

And the aircraft over the Atlantic, which had no nearest airport. About 500 were heading for
North America, entering Canadian airspace at one or two a minute. Roughly 270 were turned
back to Europe. Between 225 and 240 landed in Canada across seventeen airports — the exact
count depends on whether you use Transport Canada's or NAV CANADA's, and both are official.
Halifax took the most aircraft. Vancouver took the most passengers. And Gander, a town of
9,651 people with a runway built in the 1930s because it was the last dry land before the
ocean, took 38 aircraft and 6,656 people. The airport had been made redundant by the jet
age. That morning the redundancy was the point.

A note on who gave the order, because the record genuinely disagrees with itself. The 9/11
Commission credits Ben Sliney at 9:42 and does not mention Transportation Secretary Norman
Mineta in connection with it at all. Mineta testified under oath that he gave the order at
about 9:45. The FAA's own institutional timeline, to this day, reads "at the direction of
Transportation Secretary Norman Mineta." Both men gave a landing order within three minutes
of each other; one was the operational call on the floor, the other the Secretary's
ratification from the bunker. This film presents all three records and resolves none of
them. Mineta died in 2022, and nothing here suggests he claimed anything dishonestly.

## HONESTY NOTES (enforced in the film — keep them in packaging)
- **"About 4,500," never "4,546."** See the title note.
- **"Under three hours," never "two."** Sky clear at 12:16.
- **Sliney did not invoke SCATANA** — many videos say he did; that declaration belonged to
  the military and the Commission never says it happened.
- **Gander did not take the most planes.** Halifax did by aircraft, Vancouver by passengers.
  Gander is the story because of the ratio to town size.
- **Operation Yellow Ribbon was the code name for a tracking system built that day**, not a
  standing plan that was activated.
- **Korean Air 85 complied.** It did not refuse — the compliance is what looked like a
  hijack signal.
- **No footage of Gander in September 2001 appears in this film because none exists under a
  free licence**, and Canadian Crown copyright blocks Transport Canada's own photographs for
  a monetised video. The act is carried by narration and an original graphic. The film says
  so out loud rather than faking it.
- **Come From Away** is a musical with composite characters; it won one Tony, for direction.
  Cite it as a musical, never as history.
