# Fact Brief — "What's Actually Inside a Black Box?"
## Bucket: HISTORY & NAME ORIGIN

Confidence key: **CONFIRMED** (multiple reputable sources agree) · **PROBABLE** (well-attested, one strong source or minor variance) · **DISPUTED** (sources conflict, or it's folklore).

---

### 1. David Warren — biography & the crash that shaped him

- **Full name / dates: CONFIRMED.** David Ronald de Mey Warren, born 20 March 1925 on Groote Eylandt, Northern Territory; died 19 July 2010 in Melbourne, aged 85. His coffin was reportedly labeled "Flight Recorder Inventor; Do Not Open." [Wikipedia; CNN]
- **Father's death: CONFIRMED (aircraft name & date verified).** His father, Rev. Hubert Warren, was among 11 killed when the de Havilland DH.86 airliner *Miss Hobart* crashed into Bass Strait on **19 October 1934**, flying Launceston (Tasmania) toward Melbourne. David was **9 years old**. The wreckage was never recovered and the cause was never determined — a factual point worth stressing on-screen, because it mirrors exactly the "information void" his invention was built to close. [Wikipedia; ethw.org]
  - *Note:* The last gift from his father was a crystal radio set, often cited as sparking his interest in electronics. **PROBABLE** (widely repeated, biographical/anecdotal).
  - *Honesty flag:* Warren himself framed the connection between his father's death and his invention only later in life; treat "he vowed as a boy to fix this" as **narrative embellishment**, not documented childhood intent.
- **Role at ARL: CONFIRMED.** Warren worked at the **Aeronautical Research Laboratories (ARL)** in Melbourne from 1952 to 1983, rising to Principal Research Scientist. His day job was fuel/aircraft chemistry (jet fuels), not recorders — the recorder was a side project. [Wikipedia; Engineers Australia heritage nomination]

### 2. The ARL "Flight Memory Unit" prototype

- **Origin idea, 1953: CONFIRMED.** While on the panel investigating the world's first jetliner (de Havilland Comet) crashes, Warren realized a recording of the cockpit could solve otherwise unexplainable accidents. The concept crystallized after he saw a small pocket recorder (a "Minifon") at a trade fair. [Wikipedia; AAHOF; Engineers Australia]
- **Prototype build, 1956–1958: CONFIRMED.** He built the first prototype, the **"ARL Flight Memory Unit,"** around 1956–1957; a working unit was demonstrated by 1958. Recording medium was fine **steel wire**; it stored up to **4 hours** of voice plus instrument readings (airspeed, altitude, etc.) on a continuous loop that erased and re-recorded. [Wikipedia; AAHOF; Engineers Australia]
- **Why Australia dismissed it: CONFIRMED, quotes verified verbatim.** Response was flat. The RAAF reportedly concluded such a device "**would yield more expletives than explanations.**" Civil aviation officials said "**Dr Warren's instrument has little immediate direct use in civil aircraft.**" Pilots'/union objections (surveillance concerns) are also documented. [Wikipedia; AOPA; IEEE Spectrum — note the IEEE headline "told to drop the idea and get on with blowing up fuel tanks" reflects a real ARL directive to refocus on his fuel work.]
- **Why the UK championed it first: CONFIRMED.** In **1958**, Sir **Robert Hardingham**, secretary of the British Air Registration Board, visited ARL, grasped the significance, and arranged for Warren to demonstrate the unit in the UK, where it drew serious interest. [Wikipedia; AAHOF]
  - *Sequencing flag:* Despite British enthusiasm, **Australia became the first country to mandate cockpit voice recorders (1960)**, after a 1960 Queensland crash (Fokker F27 at Mackay) and a coroner's recommendation. So "UK adopted it first" is an oversimplification — **DISPUTED/nuanced**: the UK *championed* it early; Australia *mandated* it first. State it that way on-screen.

### 3. When FDRs and CVRs were mandated (US and elsewhere)

- **US FDR: CONFIRMED.** The **Civil Aeronautics Board (CAB)** adopted rules in **1957** requiring flight data recorders, effective **1958**, on aircraft over 12,500 lb operating above 25,000 ft; amended 1959 (60-day retention, record takeoff-to-landing). [Wikipedia; FAA/NTSB; Grossi "Aviation Recorder Overview"]
- **US CVR: CONFIRMED.** First US CVR rules passed **1964**, with compliance for turbine and 4+ piston-engine transport aircraft phased in by **1 July 1966** (turbine) / **1 March 1967**. [Wikipedia; NTSB]
- **Australia: CONFIRMED.** First nation to mandate CVRs, **1960**. [Wikipedia]
- *Note:* The FAA (created 1958) inherited/continued these rules from the CAB — attribute the earliest FDR rule to the **CAB**, not the FAA, to stay accurate.

### 4. The "black box" name — orange, not black

Flight recorders are painted **international / fluorescent orange** (mandated so wreckage searchers can spot them), so the name is a misnomer. The origin is genuinely **DISPUTED**; present the leading theories honestly:

- **WWII British electronics jargon — BEST DOCUMENTED (PROBABLE→CONFIRMED as the primary origin).** "Black box" was WWII-era British slang for the (often secret) radio/radar/navigation units in Allied aircraft, sometimes in dark housings. Earliest print cite: *Flight* magazine, **May 1946**, discussing radar "black boxes." By 1958 the term was "in colloquial use by experts." This is the origin with actual paper-trail evidence. [Wikipedia — Flight recorder]
- **"Opaque instrument" / engineering jargon — PROBABLE.** In engineering, a "black box" is any device you judge by inputs and outputs without seeing the internals — a plausible reinforcing sense, though not proven to be *the* coinage. [general engineering usage]
- **Journalist coinage / "Red Egg" story — PROBABLE but anecdotal.** Warren's own prototype was nicknamed the "Red Egg." One account has a journalist, on seeing it, exclaiming "this is a wonderful black box." Charming, single-thread anecdote — present as a story, **not** as the settled origin. [Wikipedia; Aviation24.be]
- **"Dark room" theory — SPECULATION.** Claim that early recorders needed a light-tight interior. Repeated in listicles; no strong sourcing. Flag as folk-explanation.
- **Charred-black-after-fire theory — FOLKLORE / EXCLUDE as fact.** The popular idea that they're called "black" because they emerge charred from crashes is **not supported** by reputable sources and is contradicted by the WWII paper trail predating any such usage. Mention only to debunk.
